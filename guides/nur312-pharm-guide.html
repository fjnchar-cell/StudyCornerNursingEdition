Part AEndocrineWeek 8 · Ch. 30–35

Pituitary Drugs

Ch. 30
Growth hormone — Somatropin

Somatropin stimulates overall growth and protein production and decreases glucose use. Uses: GH deficiency, AIDS wasting, pediatric growth failure.

Complications: hyperglycemia and hypercalciuria (renal calculi) — monitor for flank pain, increase water intake, decrease calcium intake. Rotate IM/SQ injection sites; obtain baseline height and weight.

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) — Vasopressin / Desmopressin

ADH (vasopressin, desmopressin) promotes water reabsorption in the kidneys via vasoconstriction. Used to treat diabetes insipidus (DI) and for vasoconstriction in hypotension.

Exam

Therapeutic effect in DI = decreased thirst (and decreased urine output). Complications: water intoxication/fluid overload, pounding headache, myocardial ischemia. Weigh daily, monitor BP, sodium, and kidney function.

Thyroid Drugs

Ch. 31
The thyroid secretes T4 (thyroxine), T3 (triiodothyronine), and calcitonin — essential for metabolism.
Read the labs Hyperthyroid: TSH low, T4 high. Hypothyroid: TSH high, T4 low. Treatment goal is to normalize both.
Hyperthyroid — Thionamides

Methimazole (MMZ) & propylthiouracil (PTU) block synthesis of thyroid hormone / conversion of T4→T3 (treats Graves'). Other options: radioactive iodine (I-131), surgery, potassium iodide.

Exam

Agranulocytosis is the key risk — report sore throat, fever, chills, malaise; monitor CBC for leukopenia/thrombocytopenia. Also risk of liver injury. Avoid iodine-rich foods.

Pregnancy: PTU in the first trimester, MMZ in the second and third. A beta blocker may be added for heart-rate control. Effects take 1–2 weeks (full effect up to 12 weeks).

Hypothyroid — Thyroid replacement
Exam

Levothyroxine (Synthroid) — pure T4, replacement for hypothyroidism. Take in the morning on an empty stomach; lifelong therapy; full effect 6–8 weeks; reassess TSH at 3–6 months.

Cautions: monitor cardiac function (palpitations, dysrhythmias). Toxicity = thyrotoxicosis / thyroid storm. Contraindicated in thyrotoxicosis and after recent MI; not used for obesity. Older adults and cardiac patients start at lower doses.

Brand switching: do not switch brands or stop without the prescriber — the correct nurse response is to check with the provider and monitor the patient. Interactions: warfarin bleeding risk; insulin and digoxin become less effective.

Antidiabetic Drugs

Ch. 32
Diabetes is self-management supported by education, medication, monitoring, and lifestyle. Insulin lowers glucose, promotes protein synthesis and lipogenesis, and shifts potassium and magnesium into cells.
Diagnosis & the 3 P's Dx: fasting glucose > 126 mg/dL or HbA1C ≥ 6.5%. Classic signs — Polyuria, Polydipsia, Polyphagia, plus glycosuria, weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision.

Type 1 vs Type 2: Type 1 (<10%) = no/defective insulin production → needs exogenous insulin. Type 2 (~90%) = insulin deficiency and/or resistance; tied to obesity, HTN, dyslipidemia, clotting risk.

Gestational diabetes: hyperglycemia in pregnancy — insulin only, no oral agents. Usually resolves after delivery; ~30% develop type 2 within 10–15 years.

Nursing priority

Check blood glucose before giving insulin, and confirm the patient will eat within the appropriate time. If a rapid-acting dose was given and the meal is delayed, give the patient food (e.g., cereal, milk, juice) rather than waiting.

Sliding-scale insulin is based on the current glucose reading and adjusts up as glucose rises. It does not replace scheduled insulin — combine only when the order says to. Downside: it reacts to highs rather than preventing them.

Oral antidiabetics
Exam

Metformin (biguanide) — first-line for type 2. Decreases hepatic glucose production and intestinal absorption; does not cause hypoglycemia (weight neutral). GI effects, metallic taste, low B12. Rare but lethal: lactic acidosis. Take with meals; hold around contrast dye studies.

Sulfonylureas (glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride) and meglitinides (repaglinide, nateglinide) stimulate insulin release from the pancreas → significant hypoglycemia risk.

Others: Glitazones (pioglitazone) decrease insulin resistance — HF risk. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors (acarbose) delay glucose absorption, take with first bite. DPP-IV "gliptins" (sitagliptin) and SGLT2 "gliflozins" (empagliflozin — yeast infections) round out the classes.

Injectable non-insulins & hypoglycemia

GLP-1 agonists (dulaglutide/Trulicity, semaglutide/Ozempic) enhance glucose-driven insulin secretion + weight loss; nausea/vomiting and rare pancreatitis. Amylin agonist pramlintide (Symlin) slows gastric emptying.

Exam

Hypoglycemia = glucose < 70 mg/dL. Early: confusion, irritability, tremor, sweating. Late: hypothermia, seizures, coma. Conscious & can swallow → fast carbs/oral glucose. Unconscious → NPO; give IV D50W or glucagon (roll to side).

Hypoglycemia "Cold and clammy — needs some candy." A patient who is cold, clammy, shaky, and confused is low, not high.

HbA1C reflects glucose control over the previous ~3 months (compliance), not the current reading. Stress, infection, illness, trauma, and corticosteroids raise blood glucose.

Adrenal Drugs

Ch. 33
The adrenal cortex secretes corticosteroids. Oversecretion → Cushing's syndrome; undersecretion → Addison's disease.

Glucocorticoids (prednisone, dexamethasone, hydrocortisone, methylprednisolone) control inflammation; mineralocorticoid fludrocortisone manages fluid/electrolytes. Used for adrenal insufficiency, inflammation, allergic reactions.

Patient teaching

Take once-daily corticosteroids in the morning to reduce adrenal suppression, take with food/milk, and never stop abruptly — taper. Do not use with active infection; report fever/sore throat.

Adverse effects: hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, hypernatremia (sodium/water retention), osteoporosis, GI bleeding/peptic ulcer, adrenal suppression, and Cushing's features (weight gain, hirsutism, bruising, muscle atrophy). Monitor daily weight and glucose.

Women's & Men's Health

Ch. 34–35

Contraceptives (oral taken at the same time daily, IUD, IM injection) prevent pregnancy. Contraindications: blood clots, HTN. Adverse effects: blood clots, dizziness, headache, depression, weight gain.

Testosterone / androgens maintain male sex characteristics, bone, and muscle (topical, IM/SQ, oral). Contraindicated in cardiac and liver impairment. Men's health also uses alpha-1 blockers (BPH), finasteride (baldness/BPH), and ED drugs (hypotension risk with alpha blockers or nitrates).

Part BCardiovascularWeek 9 · Ch. 22–27

Antihypertensive Drugs

Ch. 22
BP = CO × SVR. Hypertension is essential/primary (unknown cause, ~90%) or secondary (known cause, ~10%) and damages the heart, brain, kidneys, eyes, and vessels.
The 7 categories Diuretics · Adrenergics · Vasodilators · ACE inhibitors · ARBs · Calcium channel blockers · Direct renin inhibitors.
Nursing priority

Assess blood pressure before administering any antihypertensive; the key response to watch for with an alpha-adrenergic drug is hypotension. Teach slow position changes to prevent orthostatic syncope.

Adrenergic drugs

Adrenergic drugs cause orthostatic hypotension, first-dose syncope, dry mouth, sedation, constipation, edema, sexual dysfunction, and rebound HTN if stopped abruptly. Additive CNS depression with alcohol/benzos/opioids.

Clonidine (centrally acting) decreases sympathetic outflow; oral or patch, not first-line, do not stop abruptly. Also used for opioid withdrawal.

Alpha-1 blockers (doxazosin, prazosin, terazosin) dilate arteries/veins and improve urinary flow. Tamsulosin (Flomax) is for BPH, not BP.

Beta blockers block beta-1 → decreased SVR; taper over 1–2 weeks. Carvedilol (Coreg) treats HTN and mild-moderate HF with digoxin/diuretics/ACE; avoid in asthma/bronchospasm, severe bradycardia, cardiogenic shock.

RAAS drugs — ACE inhibitors & ARBs

ACE inhibitors ("-pril": lisinopril, enalapril, captopril, ramipril) block angiotensin I→II, reducing vasoconstriction and aldosterone-driven Na/water retention. Reduce preload & afterload, prevent ventricular remodeling after MI, and lower HF mortality.

Exam

ACE inhibitors are the drug of choice in diabetes — they reduce glomerular filtration pressure and proteinuria, preventing diabetic nephropathy (that's why a diabetic without HTN may still be prescribed lisinopril).

Exam

ACE adverse effects: dry, nonproductive cough (reverses off the drug), hyperkalemia (check potassium), and angioedema — rare but potentially fatal.

ARBs ("-sartan": losartan, valsartan, olmesartan) block angiotensin II receptors on vascular smooth muscle and adrenal gland → less vasoconstriction and aldosterone. No dry cough, hyperkalemia less likely; contraindicated in pregnancy.

CCBs, diuretics & vasodilators

Calcium channel blockers relax smooth muscle → ↓SVR and BP. Uses: HTN (amlodipine is first-line), angina, atrial fibrillation, migraine, Raynaud's, and preventing cerebral artery spasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage. Nifedipine is safe in pregnancy.

Thiazide diuretics (first-line in JNC 8) decrease plasma/ECF volume → ↓preload, CO, peripheral resistance, and BP.

Vasodilators: hydralazine relaxes arteriolar/venous smooth muscle (oral for essential HTN, injectable for emergencies; BiDil for HF in African-American patients). Sodium nitroprusside (Nipride) is IV-titrated in the ICU for severe hypertensive emergencies.

Patient teaching

Do not double missed doses or stop abruptly (rebound crisis, stroke). Give oral forms with meals; use an IV pump for IV forms. Report SOB, swelling, weight change, chest pain, palpitations, or excessive fatigue. Impotence is an expected effect that affects compliance.

Antianginal Drugs

Ch. 23
Angina = chest pain from reduced oxygen supply vs demand (chronic stable, unstable, Prinzmetal/vasospastic). Goal: increase blood flow or decrease oxygen demand. Drug classes: nitrates/nitrites, beta blockers, CCBs.

Nitrates & nitrites relax smooth muscle and dilate coronary arteries → more oxygen to ischemic myocardium. SL, IV, transdermal, ointment, and translingual forms bypass first-pass metabolism. Rapid-acting = acute attacks; long-acting = prevention.

Safety

Never combine nitrates with ED drugs — sildenafil (Viagra), tadalafil (Cialis), vardenafil (Levitra) → severe, dangerous hypotension. Also contraindicated in severe anemia, closed-angle glaucoma, and severe head injury.

Nitrate adverse effects: headache (diminishes with use), reflex tachycardia, postural hypotension, topical skin irritation, and possible tolerance.

Heart Failure Drugs

Ch. 24
Heart failure = the heart can't pump enough to meet the body's needs → insufficient cardiac output and fluid overload (left, right, or both). Therapy uses ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta blockers, diuretics, and inotropes.
The three "-tropics" Inotropic = force of contraction · Chronotropic = heart rate · Dromotropic = conduction. Digoxin is +inotropic, −chronotropic, −dromotropic.

Dobutamine — a positive inotrope; beta-1-selective vasoactive adrenergic that increases contraction force and blood ejected.

Exam

Digoxin (Lanoxin): check apical pulse for 1 full minute; hold if < 60. Therapeutic level 0.5–2 ng/mL; standard dose 0.125–0.25 mg daily. Improves symptoms and exercise tolerance but does not reduce mortality.

Exam

Digoxin toxicity: bradycardia, headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, and visual disturbances (yellow halos). Low potassium or magnesium raises toxicity risk (so a K⁺ of 2.0 is the red flag, not an apical pulse of 62). Antidote: digoxin immune Fab (Digibind).

Antidysrhythmic Drugs

Ch. 25
Dysrhythmia = any deviation from normal rhythm (a-fib, a-flutter, SVT, VT, VF). Vaughan Williams classes: I (Na⁺ channel), II (beta blockers), III (amiodarone), IV (CCBs).

Amiodarone (Class III) prolongs the action potential; drug of choice for ventricular dysrhythmias (sustained VT, VF) per ACLS. Adverse: corneal microdeposits/visual halos, photophobia, dry eyes, photosensitivity, and pulmonary toxicity.

Exam

Diltiazem (Cardizem, Class IV CCB) slows AV conduction for PSVT and rate control in a-flutter/fib; also angina, HTN, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Risk of hypotension — if a patient reports fatigue and dizziness, the priority is to check the blood pressure.

Adenosine (Adenocard) for PSVT can cause a brief asystole on IV push — a transient, expected pause; if asystole persists, begin CPR.

Coagulation Modifiers

Ch. 26
Who does what Anticoagulants prevent new clots · Antiplatelets block platelet aggregation · Thrombolytics lyse existing clots · Antifibrinolytics promote clot formation. A thrombus is a clot; an embolus is one that travels (→ MI, CVA, PE, DVT).

Heparin inhibits factors IIa (thrombin) and Xa. Do not aspirate or massage SQ sites; give in deep SQ fat away from the umbilicus. Watch for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) → treat with argatroban/lepirudin. Enoxaparin (Lovenox) is LMWH — no routine labs, don't expel the syringe air bubble.

Warfarin takes several days for full effect, so heparin overlaps warfarin (~5 days) until the INR is therapeutic. Many drug/herbal interactions; keep vitamin K foods (leafy greens, spinach) consistent — don't binge or avoid entirely. Newer orals: direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran (Pradaxa, antidote idarucizumab) and factor Xa inhibitor rivaroxaban (Xarelto).

Heparin toxicity / bleeding: hematuria, melena, petechiae, ecchymoses, gum/mucous-membrane bleeding → stop the drug; 1 mg protamine reverses ~100 units of heparin. On warfarin, an elderly patient who falls and hits their head needs assessment for internal bleeding.

Antiplatelets, thrombolytics & antifibrinolytics

Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel/Plavix, eptifibatide) prevent platelet adhesion at vessel injury. Bleeding is the main adverse effect; combining with ginkgo raises bleeding risk.

Thrombolytics ("-ase": alteplase/tPA, reteplase, tenecteplase) activate plasminogen → plasmin to lyse existing clots. Uses: acute MI, ischemic stroke, PE, DVT. Big risk = bleeding (internal, intracranial); requires ICU monitoring ≥ 24 h.

Antifibrinolytics (aminocaproic acid/Amicar, tranexamic acid/TXA, desmopressin/DDAVP) promote clotting — for hemophilia, von Willebrand's, surgical/postpartum hemorrhage.

GI Drugs

Acid · Laxatives
Acid-controlling drugs and laxatives. The stomach protects itself with mucous cells that secrete a mucus–bicarbonate layer over the lining; ulcer drugs either cut acid or reinforce that barrier.
Protecting the stomach & acid control
Exam

Mucous cells protect the stomach lining from acid — they secrete a layer of mucus and bicarbonate that buffers hydrochloric acid and keeps it off the mucosa.

Exam

Sucralfate (Carafate) therapeutic effect: it forms a protective paste/barrier over the ulcer site, shielding it from acid and pepsin so it can heal. Give on an empty stomach before meals; it can bind other drugs, so separate their timing.

Exam

PPIs in the ICU: proton pump inhibitors (e.g., IV pantoprazole) are used for stress ulcer prophylaxis — preventing stress-related mucosal bleeding in critically ill patients.

PPIs ("-prazole", omeprazole) block the H⁺/K⁺ pump — the final step of acid secretion. Teach: take before meals, swallow whole (don't crush), and note that long-term use risks low magnesium, B12, and fracture. Also on the list: H₂ blockers ("-tidine") and antacids that neutralize existing acid.

Laxatives
Exam

Labs to monitor on laxatives: electrolytes — especially potassium, plus sodium and magnesium — and fluid balance. Fluid and electrolyte loss (hypokalemia, dehydration) is the main risk with overuse.

Exam

Magnesium hydroxide (Milk of Magnesia) adverse effects: diarrhea (osmotic effect) and, especially with renal impairment, hypermagnesemia — plus cramping and dehydration.

Exam

Long-term laxative use leads to laxative dependence and loss of normal bowel tone/function, along with chronic fluid and electrolyte imbalance.

Blood-Forming Drugs

Hematopoietic
Self-administration

Epoetin alfa (Epogen, Procrit) stimulates red-cell production for anemia. Self-injection steps at home: wash hands and gather supplies; check the dose and expiration; do not shake the vial (shaking inactivates it); give subcutaneously, rotating sites; use a new needle each time and dispose in a sharps container; store in the refrigerator.

Monitoring: check blood pressure (hypertension is a key risk) and hemoglobin/hematocrit regularly. Report headache or chest pain; do not overshoot the target hemoglobin because too-high levels raise the risk of clots.

Neoplastic

Common chemotherapy effects: nausea, vomiting, fatigue, anorexia, and pain.
