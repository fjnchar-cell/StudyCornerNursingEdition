Approach

The exam is a sequence, not a checklist

A head‑to‑toe exam is a rehearsed arrangement of skills you already know — not new content. A few points the deck makes about the sequence itself:

  • You may pause mid‑exam to think through what comes next, rather than gathering data on autopilot. That's normal early on.
  • Repetition is what makes the sequence flow naturally — this is a motor‑skill problem as much as a knowledge problem.
  • If you forget a step, insert it gracefully wherever you remember it. A missed step out of order still counts.
  • The suggested order below is built to minimize position changes for both you and the patient — that's the organizing logic behind why regions are grouped the way they are.

Opening the exam

  • Patient walks into the room and sits; you sit facing the patient. The patient stays in street clothes for this first portion.
  • Review your interview technique/strategies before you start gathering data.
  • Obtain the health history, then observe general appearance throughout.
  • Take measurements: height and weight. Weigh the patient the same way each day (same scale, similar clothing, similar time) so weights are comparable.

Symptom vs. sign

Symptom — subjective. What the patient tells you (pain, nausea, dizziness).
Sign — objective. What you detect on exam (a rash, a murmur, a fever you measured).

IPPA — the exam order

Inspection → Palpation → Percussion → Auscultation.
Exception: for the abdomen, auscultate before palpating/percussing — pressing on the belly first can change bowel sounds.

Palpation technique changes with what you're feeling for — fingertips for texture/pulses, ulnar/palmar surface for vibration (fremitus, thrills), dorsal surface for temperature. Hand hygiene before and after every patient contact, every time.

Approach

General survey & the health history

The health history is an interview — collected complete or limited, depending on what the visit warrants. While you take it, you're simultaneously screening general appearance; the two aren't separate steps. Know the purpose of each database type (complete vs. episodic vs. follow-up vs. emergency) — this shows up as "why would you choose this type of assessment for this patient" style questions.

General appearance — what you're screening

  • Appears stated age, and level of consciousness (LOC)
  • Skin color, nutritional status, personal hygiene
  • Posture — comfortably erect; watch for physical deformities
  • Mobility
  • Facial expression, mood/affect, hearing
  • Speech: articulation, pattern, content appropriate to context, native language

General survey — the four buckets

CategoryWhat's assessed
Physical appearanceAge, sex, LOC, skin color, facial features, overall appearance
Body structureStature, nutrition, symmetry, posture, position
MobilityGait, range of motion (ROM)
BehaviorFacial expressions, mood/affect, speech, dress, personal hygiene
Skin & Vitals

Measurement, skin & nails

Measurement

Weight and height → compute BMI. Know the silhouette contrast the deck flags: Marfan syndrome (tall, long limbs/fingers, disproportionate arm span) vs. dwarfism (short stature, disproportionate limb-to-trunk ratio) — a classic "spot the body-habitus difference" exam item.

Skin vocabulary from the deck

Chancre sore
Deck's definition: a superficial ulcer with yellowish discharge, on the mouth.
Crepitus
A coarse, crackling sensation felt over the surface of the skin (classically, air trapped under the skin).
Pale / cyanosis / erythema / jaundice
Know where to assess each — pallor and cyanosis show best in nail beds, lips, and mucous membranes; jaundice shows best in the sclera and under the tongue; erythema is easiest to see in non-pigmented skin and may need palpation (blanching) to confirm in darker skin tones.
Worth double‑checking

A true chancre (as in a syphilitic chancre) is a painless genital ulcer, not an oral one. What the deck describes — a small, painful oral ulcer with a yellowish base — matches a canker sore (aphthous ulcer) far more closely. The two terms get mixed up often; if a question distinguishes them, "canker sore" is the oral one.

Skin lesions — primary vs. secondary

Primary (arise from normal skin)

  • Macule — flat, flush with skin, color change only (e.g., a freckle)
  • Papule — raised, solid, <1 cm
  • Vesicle — raised, fluid-filled, <1 cm (e.g., herpes)
  • Blister / bulla — raised, fluid-filled, >1 cm
  • Wheal — raised, irregular, transient (e.g., an insect bite)
  • Urticaria — coalesced wheals (hives)
  • Cyst — elevated, encapsulated, filled with liquid or semisolid material

Secondary (evolve from a primary lesion)

  • Ulcer — a depressed lesion; skin loss extending into the dermis, may bleed or scar

This is the core vocabulary tested on skin questions — being able to name a lesion from its description (raised vs. flat, fluid-filled vs. solid, size, duration) is the actual skill.

Mnemonic

ABCDE(F) — red flags for a lesion that might be melanoma:

  • Asymmetry
  • Border irregularity
  • Color variation within the lesion
  • Diameter > 6 mm (pencil-eraser size)
  • Evolving — changing in size, shape, or color
  • (Funny-looking — stands out from the patient's other moles, the "ugly duckling" sign)

Nails — clubbing angle

The nail-to-finger angle is the whole test. Watch the direction it moves: larger angle = more clubbing = more concerning (chronic hypoxia — think advanced lung or cyanotic heart disease).

FindingAngleMeaning
Normal160°Normal profile sign
Curved nail160° or lessStill within/near normal — a curvier nail bed, not true clubbing
Early clubbing180°Angle flattens/exceeds 180° — early clubbing, work up for chronic hypoxia
Skin & Vitals

Vital signs, head & face

Vital signs

  • Radial pulse
  • Respirations
  • Blood pressure — in the arms, or lower legs if indicated (e.g., suspected coarctation, or when arms are unusable)
  • Temperature

Head & face

  • Inspect and palpate the scalp, hair, and cranium.
  • Inspect the face for expression and symmetry — this is your CN VII (facial) screen.
  • Palpate the temporal artery, then the TMJ as the patient opens and closes their mouth.
  • Palpate the maxillary and frontal sinuses. If tender, transilluminate them (a lit sinus glows red/pink; fluid or thickened mucosa blocks the light).
HEENT

Eyes

Exam sequence

  • Test visual fields by confrontation → CN II
  • Extraocular muscles: corneal light reflex + the six cardinal positions of gaze → CN III, IV, VI
  • Inspect external eye structures; inspect conjunctivae, sclerae, corneas
  • Test pupil size for light and accommodation
  • Darken the room, then use the ophthalmoscope to inspect the ocular fundus — red reflex, optic disc, vessels, retinal background
Mnemonic

PERRLA — Pupils Equal, Round, Reactive to Light and Accommodation.

Accommodation
The eye's lens adapting for near vision.
Convergence
Both eyeballs' axes moving inward, toward each other, to focus on a near object.
Pupillary constriction
The direct/consensual response to light — CN III (oculomotor) efferent pathway.
6 cardinal gazes
Screens CN III, IV, and VI together by moving the eyes through the 6 directions where a single muscle/nerve dominates.
Snellen chart
Standard visual-acuity test; recorded as a fraction (e.g., 20/20) comparing the patient's distance vision to a "normal" eye's.
HEENT

Ears

  • Inspect the external ear: position/alignment, skin condition, auditory meatus.
  • Move the auricle and push on the tragus — checks for tenderness (a positive finding suggests otitis externa, not otitis media).
  • Otoscope: inspect the canal, then the tympanic membrane (TM) for color, position, landmarks, and integrity.
  • Test hearing with a voice test, then Weber and Rinne.

Weber & Rinne — reading the results

TestHow it's doneNormalAbnormal
Weber Tuning fork on the midline of the skull (forehead or vertex) Heard equally in both ears Lateralizes to the ear with a conductive loss; lateralizes away from (to the good ear, for) a sensorineural loss
Rinne Fork on the mastoid (bone conduction) until sound fades, then moved beside the ear canal (air conduction) Air conduction > bone conduction ("positive Rinne") Bone conduction > air conduction ("negative Rinne") = conductive loss
HEENT

Nose

  • Inspect the external nose for symmetry and lesions; recheck facial symmetry (CN VII).
  • Test patency of each nostril individually.
  • With a nasal speculum: inspect the nares — mucosa, septum, turbinates.
  • Note septal perforation if present, and distinguish septal deviation (structural shift, usually developmental or traumatic) from a tear (acute injury).

Halo sign — CSF leak

Drip clear or blood-tinged nasal/ear drainage onto filter paper or gauze. If it separates into a central red (blood) ring with a lighter, clear ring around it, that's a positive halo sign — it suggests the drainage contains cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), most often after head trauma. This is an urgent finding, not a routine one.

HEENT

Mouth & throat

  • Penlight exam: buccal mucosa, teeth and gums, tongue, floor of mouth, palate, uvula.
  • Grade tonsils if present (see below).
  • Ask the patient to say "ahh" — watch uvula mobility/symmetry and test the gag reflex → CN IX & CN X.
  • Ask the patient to stick out their tongue → CN XII.
  • Gloved, bimanual palpation of the mouth if indicated.

Tonsil grading

GradePosition
1+Tonsils visible, tucked within the tonsillar pillars (normal)
2+Tonsils extend to the pillars
3+Tonsils extend past the pillars, toward the uvula
4+Tonsils touch each other at the midline — "kissing tonsils"
HEENT

Neck

  • Inspect the neck for symmetry, lumps, and pulsations.
  • Palpate cervical lymph nodes.
  • Palpate the carotid pulse one side at a time — never both together, this can cut cerebral blood flow. If indicated, auscultate for carotid bruits.
  • Palpate the trachea in the midline.
  • Test ROM and muscle strength against resistance: head forward/back, head turned each way, shoulder shrug → CN XI.
  • Step behind the patient (bring your stethoscope, ruler, and marking pen) and palpate the thyroid gland from the posterior approach.
  • Open the gown to expose the full back for the thorax exam, leaving it on the shoulders and anterior chest for now.
Chest & Lungs

Thorax, lungs & breath sounds

Posterior & lateral chest

  • Inspect the thoracic cage's configuration, skin characteristics, and symmetry of shoulders/muscles.
  • Palpate for symmetric expansion, tactile fremitus, lumps/tenderness, and the length of the spinous processes.
  • Percuss over all lung fields (diaphragmatic excursion) and the costovertebral angle (CVA tenderness = possible kidney involvement).
  • Auscultate breath sounds; note any adventitious sounds.
  • Move around to face the patient (who stays sitting). For a female breast exam, ask permission to lift the gown onto the shoulders to expose the anterior chest; for a male, lower the gown to the lap.

Anterior chest

  • Inspect respirations and skin characteristics.
  • Palpate for tactile fremitus, lumps, or tenderness.
  • Percuss the anterior lung fields.
  • Auscultate breath sounds.

Thoracic cavity shapes

Scoliosis
Lateral curvature of the spine — causes asymmetric rib cage/shoulder height.
Barrel chest
Increased anteroposterior (AP) diameter, roughly equal to the transverse diameter (normal ratio is about 1:2, AP:transverse). Classic in COPD from chronic air-trapping.
Kyphosis
Exaggerated outward (posterior) curve of the thoracic spine — a hunched upper back.

Know your percussion sounds for the lungs: resonance (normal lung), hyperresonance (air-trapping — COPD, pneumothorax), dullness (consolidation, fluid, mass).

Heart & Vascular

Heart exam & valves

Exam sequence

  • Inspect the precordium for pulsations or a heave (lift).
  • Palpate: locate the apical impulse; feel the precordium for any abnormal thrill.
  • Auscultate: apical rate and rhythm. Use the diaphragm to study heart sounds, inching from apex up to base (or base to apex). Then switch to the bell and inch through all locations again — the bell picks up low-pitched sounds the diaphragm can miss (S3, S4, mitral stenosis rumble).
  • Turn the patient onto their left side and auscultate the apex again with the bell — this brings the apex closer to the chest wall and accentuates low-pitched/left-sided sounds.
  • Note the location of murmurs and heart sounds, and think through blood-flow direction and the heart's electrical conduction direction as you go — both help you reason out where an abnormal sound is coming from.
Mnemonic

APE to MAN — the auscultation points, top to bottom of the chest:

  • Aortic valve — 2nd right intercostal space, sternal border
  • Pulmonic valve — 2nd left intercostal space, sternal border
  • Erb's point — 3rd left intercostal space, sternal border. Not a valve — the single best spot to hear the whole heart / murmurs at one time
  • Tricuspid valve — 4th–5th left intercostal space, sternal border
  • Mitral valve — 5th left intercostal space, midclavicular line (the apex)
S1
First heart sound — closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves. Marks the start of systole.
S2
Second heart sound — closure of the pulmonic and aortic valves. Marks the start of diastole.
Base of the heart
The top — where the great vessels attach (aortic/pulmonic auscultation points).
Apex of the heart
The bottom tip — where the mitral point/apical impulse is (5th ICS, midclavicular line).
Heart & Vascular

Peripheral vascular & edema

PAD vs. PVD

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) starves the limb of blood; peripheral venous disease (PVD) lets blood pool. Opposite mechanisms, opposite findings:

Peripheral venous disease (PVD)Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)
PainDull, achyIntermittent claudication (cramping with activity, relieved by rest)
PulsePresentWeak or absent
EdemaPresent — pooling of bloodAbsent — no blood reaching the tissue
TemperatureWarmCool

Edema grading (pitting)

GradeDepthDescription
0—No pitting edema
1+2 mmMild. Depression disappears rapidly.
2+4 mmModerate. Depression disappears in 10–15 seconds.
3+6 mmModerately severe. Depression may last more than 1 minute.
4+8 mmSevere. Depression can last more than 2 minutes.

Vascular sounds & lower-extremity pulses

Auscultate for bruits over the renal, iliac, and femoral arteries — a bruit here suggests turbulent flow from a narrowing.

  • Palpate each groin for the femoral pulse and inguinal lymph nodes; lift the drape to expose the legs.
  • Palpate for temperature and pretibial edema.
  • Inspect legs for symmetry, skin characteristics, hair distribution (hair loss can signal poor arterial flow).
  • Palpate pulses: popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis.
  • Separate the toes and inspect between them. Test ROM and muscle strength of hips, knees, ankles, feet.
Heart & Vascular

Breasts & neck vessels

Female breast exam

  • Inspect for symmetry, mobility, and dimpling as the patient lifts arms overhead, pushes hands on hips, and leans forward. Breasts don't need to be 100% symmetrical to be normal.
  • Palpate each breast with the same-side arm lifted overhead. Include the tail of Spence (the breast tissue extending toward the axilla) and the areola. Check each nipple for discharge.
  • Support the patient's arm and palpate the axilla and regional lymph nodes.
  • Teach breast self-exam — and use correct positioning for palpation.
  • If you find an abnormality, always ask: is this the patient's normal, or is it new? Onset of a new symptom is the priority — it's what changes your next action.

Male breasts & neck vessels

  • Male breasts: inspect and palpate while palpating the anterior chest wall; support each arm and palpate axilla/regional nodes.
  • Neck vessels: inspect each side for a jugular venous pulse, turning the patient's head slightly away. Estimate jugular venous pressure (JVP) if indicated — an elevated JVP is a key sign of right-sided heart failure/fluid overload.
Abdomen & GI

Abdomen exam

Order matters here: inspect → auscultate → percuss → palpate — auscultation moves up before percussion/palpation so you don't disturb bowel sounds before you've heard them.

  • Inspect for contour, symmetry, skin characteristics, umbilicus, pulsations. With ascites, look for a distended, often asymmetric abdomen with bulging flanks — a specific physical finding to know.
  • Auscultate bowel sounds (know what they mean — see below) and vascular sounds over the aorta and renal arteries.
  • Percuss all quadrants; measure liver span in the right midclavicular line; locate the spleen; check for CVA tenderness.
  • Palpate: light palpation in all quadrants, then deep palpation in all quadrants. Palpate the bladder, and for the liver, spleen, kidneys, and aorta.

Bowel sounds

Hypoactive
Decreased. Can follow abdominal surgery or occur with inflammation.
Hyperactive
Loud, high-pitched, signals increased motility.
Borborygmus
The sound of hyperperistalsis — a loud, prolonged growl/gurgle.
Testable detail

A truly "silent abdomen" is uncommon. You must listen for 5 full minutes by the clock before you can document bowel sounds as completely absent.

Bright red stool = bleeding low in the GI tract (rectum). Dark red / melena-type stool = bleeding higher up (upper GI tract) — blood has been digested on the way down.

Neuro & MSK

Neurologic exam

Sensory & cerebellar testing

  • Test sensation on face, arms, hands, legs, feet: superficial pain, light touch, vibration.
  • Position sense of one finger, one hand.
  • Stereognosis — identifying an object by touch alone, eyes closed (e.g., a key in the palm).
  • Graphesthesia — identifying a number or letter traced on the palm, eyes closed.
  • Cerebellar function, upper extremities: finger-to-nose test or rapid-alternating-movements test.
  • Cerebellar function, lower extremities: heel-down-opposite-shin test.
  • Elicit deep tendon reflexes (DTRs): biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar, Achilles.
  • Test the Babinski reflex (stroking the sole) — toes fanning out/upward is abnormal in anyone past infancy, and suggests an upper motor neuron lesion.

DTR grading scale

GradeFinding
0No response (areflexia)
1+Diminished (hyporeflexia)
2+Normal / average
3+Brisker than average — not necessarily abnormal on its own
4+Very brisk, hyperactive, with clonus — hyperreflexia

Neuro & MSK

LOC, posturing & meningeal signs

Decorticate vs. decerebrate posturing

Decorticate

  • Arms: flexed, drawn in toward the core (adducted)
  • Legs: extended, feet plantar-flexed
  • Indicates damage above the brainstem (cerebral hemispheres/internal capsule)

Decerebrate

  • Arms: extended, rigid, pronated, adducted
  • Legs: extended, feet plantar-flexed
  • Indicates brainstem damage — more severe than decorticate
Memory hook

deCORTicate = arms bent toward the core. deCEREBRATE = arms extended and rigid — think "Extended" for both the posture and the emergency it represents. If a patient progresses from decorticate to decerebrate, that's worsening.

Meningeal signs

Mnemonic
  • KErnig sign — Knee Extension is painful (hip flexed to 90°, then knee extension causes pain/resistance)
  • BrudziNsKi sign — Neck flexion leads to Knee flexion (passive neck flexion causes involuntary hip/knee flexion)

Both suggest meningeal irritation — think meningitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Neuro & MSK

Musculoskeletal & gait

  • Inspect legs for varicose veins.
  • Watch the patient's regular gait, then have them walk heel-to-toe across the room.
  • Walk on toes for a few steps, then on heels for a few steps.
  • Check for Romberg sign — stand close by; the patient stands feet together, eyes closed. Significant swaying/falling is a positive Romberg, suggesting a proprioceptive or vestibular problem (not cerebellar — a cerebellar problem sways with eyes open, too).
  • Shallow knee bend, one leg at a time, holding the edge of the bed/table.
  • Check the spine as the patient touches their toes; test ROM as they hyperextend, rotate, and laterally bend.

Wrap-up

End of exam & Week 10 topics

Closing the exam

  • Tell the patient you're finished and will step out while they get dressed.
  • Return to discuss findings, plans, and answer questions.
  • Thank the patient for their time.
  • For a hospitalized patient: return the bed and any equipment to how you found it. Make sure the call light and telephone are within easy reach before you leave.

Week 10 additions

CAGE test
Alcohol-use screening tool — Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener. Two or more "yes" answers suggests further evaluation is warranted.
Mental health status
Part of the general survey/history — mood, affect, thought process, orientation.
Withdrawal
Recognize the pattern of symptoms that follow stopping a substance the body has become dependent on.
Culturally competent care
Adjust your interview and exam approach to the patient's cultural context — without assuming beliefs based on background.
Pain — subjective survey
Use a mnemonic (PQRSTU or OLDCARTS) to structure the pain history; know the difference between acute pain (recent onset, protective/warning function) and the other types of pain (chronic, nociceptive vs. neuropathic).
Practice

Practice questions

Straight from the review deck. The deck didn't mark correct answers — these are worked out from standard nursing content, with a rationale. Verify against your instructor's key before you trust them for the exam.

Question 1

The nurse is using the mnemonic PQRSTU for assessing a client's pain. Which question would be most appropriate in the "R" section?

  1. 1. "What were you doing when the pain first occurred?"
  2. 2. "Where is the pain located?"
  3. 3. "Are there certain activities that worsen the pain?"
  4. 4. "What does the pain feel like?"
Answer: 2. In PQRSTU, "R" stands for Region/Radiation — where the pain is, and where it spreads. Option 1 is Provocation ("P"), option 3 is also provocation/palliation, and option 4 is Quality ("Q").
Question 2

While performing a client's physical assessment, the nurse has changed from using the diaphragm of the stethoscope to using the bell. The nurse is most likely assessing which of the following?

  1. 1. Bowel sounds
  2. 2. Breath sounds
  3. 3. Femoral pulses
  4. 4. Heart sounds
Answer: 4. The bell is for low-pitched sounds — extra heart sounds like S3/S4 and the low rumble of mitral stenosis. Bowel and breath sounds are conventionally assessed with the diaphragm.
Question 3

Which statement is true regarding the arterial system?

  1. a. Arteries are defined as large-diameter vessels
  2. b. The walls of arteries are thinner than those of the veins
  3. c. The arterial system is a high-pressure system
  4. d. Arteries can expand greater than veins to accommodate a large blood volume increase
Answer: c. Arteries carry blood under high pressure straight from the heart, so their walls are thicker and more muscular than veins' — the opposite of options a, b, and d. Veins, not arteries, are the more distensible, higher-capacity side of the circulation.
Question 4

When using a Doppler ultrasonic stethoscope, the nurse recognizes arterial flow when which sound is heard?

  1. a. Steady, even, flowing sound
  2. b. Regular "lub, dub" pattern
  3. c. Low humming sound
  4. d. Swishing, whooshing sound
Answer: d. Arterial Doppler flow is pulsatile — a rhythmic swish/whoosh that tracks systole and diastole. A steady, continuous flowing sound (option a) is more typical of venous flow. "Lub, dub" describes auscultated heart sounds, not a Doppler signal.
Question 5

During cardiac auscultation for a 5-year-old boy, the nurse hears a sound occurring before the S1 at the fifth left intercostal space. What should the nurse do to further assess this sound?

  1. a. Ask the client to hold their breath while the nurse listens again
  2. b. Have the client turn to the left side while the nurse listens with the bell of the stethoscope
  3. c. Watch the client's respirations while listening for the effect on the sound
  4. d. No further assessment is needed because the nurse knows this sound is S3, which is an expected finding
Answer: b. A sound just before S1 is an S4, not S3 — S3 falls right after S2. Left-lateral positioning with the bell is the standard maneuver to bring out low-pitched extra sounds like S4 at the apex, so option d is wrong on two counts (wrong sound, and an S4 is not a benign expected finding the way an innocent S3 can sometimes be in children).
Question 6

The nurse is reviewing venous blood flow patterns. Which of these statements best describes the mechanism(s) by which venous blood returns to the heart?

  1. a. Increased thoracic pressure and decreased abdominal pressure facilitate venous return to the heart.
  2. b. Contracting skeletal muscles milk blood distally toward the veins.
  3. c. Intraluminal valves ensure unidirectional flow toward the heart
  4. d. High-pressure system of the heart helps facilitate venous return.
Answer: c. One-way valves inside the veins are what keep blood moving toward the heart instead of pooling. Option a has the pressure relationship backwards (it's decreased thoracic and increased abdominal pressure with inspiration that helps venous return). Option b has the direction backwards — muscle contraction milks blood proximally, toward the heart. Option d is backwards too: veins are a low-pressure system.
Question 7

The nurse is taking the history of a client who may have a perforated eardrum. What would be an important question in this situation?

  1. a. "Do you ever notice ringing or crackling in your ears?"
  2. b. "When was the last time you had your hearing checked?"
  3. c. "Have you ever been told that you have any type of hearing loss?"
  4. d. "Is there any relationship between the ear pain and the discharge you mentioned?"
Answer: d (best supported, though reasonable people could argue for "a" — worth confirming with your instructor). A classic sign of a ruptured TM is sudden relief of ear pain right as discharge (otorrhea) begins — asking about that relationship directly targets the mechanism. Options b and c ask about longstanding hearing status rather than the acute event.
Question 8

An older adult client states, "Sometimes when I sneeze, I notice that I wet my pants." How would the nurse interpret this finding?

  1. a. Urge incontinence
  2. b. Stress incontinence
  3. c. Reflex incontinence
  4. d. Total incontinence
Answer: b. Leakage triggered by a sudden rise in intra-abdominal pressure — sneezing, coughing, laughing, lifting — is the textbook definition of stress incontinence. Urge incontinence is a sudden, strong need to void that can't be suppressed in time; reflex and total incontinence involve loss of the usual sensation/control pathways altogether.
