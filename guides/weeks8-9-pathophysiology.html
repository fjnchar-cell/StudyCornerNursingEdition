Renal & Urinary System

Chapter 18 — filtration, labs, infection, failure
Kidney functions & anatomy2 cards

What the kidneys do

  • Filter metabolic waste and remove drugs, hormones, and foreign material.
  • Regulate water, electrolyte, and acid–base balance.
  • Secrete erythropoietin to stimulate red-cell production.
  • Activate vitamin D.
  • Control blood pressure through the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS).
Exam link

Because kidneys make EPO and activate vitamin D, kidney failure causes anemia and bone disease — not just fluid problems.

Anatomy & the nephron

  • Path of urine: kidneys → ureters → bladder → urethra.
  • The nephron is the functional unit; each kidney has over a million.
  • Renal corpuscle = glomerulus + Bowman capsule.
  • Tubules: proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct.
Urine formation & hormones

Three steps of urine formation

  • Filtration — fluid, wastes, nutrients, and electrolytes pass from glomerular capillaries into Bowman capsule. Cells and protein stay in the blood.
  • Reabsorption — essential nutrients, water, and electrolytes return to the peritubular capillaries; most water and all glucose are reabsorbed in the proximal tubule.
  • Secretion — controls pH and clears extra ions and drugs.

Hormones that control reabsorption

  • ADH (posterior pituitary) → reabsorbs water in the distal tubule and collecting duct.
  • Aldosterone (adrenal cortex) → reabsorbs sodium in exchange for potassium or hydrogen.
  • Atrial natriuretic hormone (heart) → reduces sodium and fluid reabsorption.
Memory hook

ADH = Add Water. Aldosterone = save salt, lose K⁺. ANH = the "anti" hormone that dumps salt and water.

GFR & its control

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR)

GFR estimates how much blood the kidneys filter per minute — the single best measure of overall kidney function.

  • Constrict the afferent arteriole → less pressure in → less filtrate.
  • Dilate the afferent arteriole → more pressure in → more filtrate.
  • Constrict the efferent arteriole → pressure backs up → more filtrate.

Three controllers of GFR

  • Autoregulation — local arteriole adjustments to changing blood flow.
  • Sympathetic nervous system — vasoconstricts both arterioles.
  • Renin — released by juxtaglomerular cells when afferent blood flow drops, triggering the renin–angiotensin mechanism.
↔ Pharmacology (NUR 312)

This is exactly where ACE inhibitors / ARBs act. They relax the efferent arteriole, lower glomerular pressure, and protect the kidney over time — the reason they reduce diabetic and glomerular proteinuria.

Labs & diagnosticsexam-heavy

Blood markers of kidney filtration

  • Creatinine — waste from normal muscle activity. A rise is the single most useful indicator of reduced filtration.
  • BUN (blood urea nitrogen) — nitrogen waste; rises with reduced clearance but also with dehydration.
  • GFR — estimates and stages filtration capacity.
Exam focus

Rising creatinine + BUN = reduced renal clearance. Always interpret with urine findings, fluid status, and medications — not in isolation.

Urinalysis — normal vs abnormal

Normal urine is straw-colored with a mild odor and should not contain protein, glucose, blood, or white cells. Specific gravity ≈ 1.010–1.030.

  • Proteinuria — glomerular damage / increased permeability.
  • Hematuria — trauma, stones, inflammation, glomerular disease.
  • Pyuria — WBCs/pus → infection.
  • Glucosuria — high blood glucose; sugar feeds bacteria.
  • Bacteriuria — supports urinary infection.
  • RBC casts — bleeding/inflammation inside the nephron → glomerular disease.
Memory hook

Casts point to the tubules. RBC casts = glomerulonephritis; WBC casts = pyelonephritis.

Diagnostic tests

  • Urine culture & sensitivity — identifies the organism and the right antibiotic.
  • Imaging — ultrasound, CT, IV pyelography.
  • Cystoscopy — scope through the urethra to view the bladder; can biopsy or remove stones.
  • Clearance tests (creatinine, inulin) assess GFR; antibody titers (ASO) support post-strep glomerulonephritis.
Word hook

Cysto- = bladder, -oscopy = look with a scope → cystoscopy views the bladder.

Urinary tract infections

UTIs — where and why

  • Urethritis = urethra · Cystitis = bladder · Ureteritis = ureter · Pyelonephritis = kidney.
  • Most common organism: E. coli.
  • More common in women — shorter urethra, closer to the anus.
  • Risk factors: urine stasis, retention, glucose in urine, reflux, catheters, prostatic enlargement in older men.

Recognizing a UTI

  • Lower tract: dysuria, urgency, frequency, nocturia, cloudy/malodorous urine, suprapubic pain.
  • Systemic: fever, malaise, nausea, leukocytosis.
  • Older adults may present with new confusion as the only sign.
  • Pyelonephritis adds flank pain and costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness.
↔ Health Assessment (NUR 315)

CVA tenderness is elicited by gently percussing the back over the kidney (fist percussion). A positive result points to pyelonephritis, not simple cystitis.

Treatment

Bacterial UTIs are treated with antibiotics; culture & sensitivity guides the choice. Adequate hydration and regular bladder emptying reduce stasis.

↔ Pharmacology (NUR 312)

Common agents: nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX (Bactrim), cephalosporins, amoxicillin. Fosfomycin is chosen in pregnancy.

Glomerulonephritis vs Nephrotic syndromehigh yield

Glomerulonephritis (nephritic picture)

Inflammation of the glomeruli that leaks blood and some protein into the urine.

  • Hematuria (dark/cloudy urine), RBC casts, proteinuria.
  • Decreased urine output, edema (periorbital/facial first), hypertension.
  • Elevated BUN/creatinine; often post-streptococcal (↑ ASO, ↓ complement).
  • No fever/bacteriuria of infection.

Nephrotic syndrome

Marked glomerular permeability to protein.

  • Massive proteinuria and hypoalbuminemia.
  • Massive/generalized edema, lipiduria (milky urine), urinary casts.
  • Hematuria is not the defining feature.
Memory hook

NephrOtic = prOtein. A question about low albumin specifically → nephrotic. A question about blood + RBC casts → glomerulonephritis.

Obstruction: stones, hydronephrosis, nephrosclerosis

Urolithiasis (kidney stones)

  • Also called renal calculi / nephrolithiasis. Most common composition is calcium.
  • Promoted by dehydration (concentrated urine), infection, and high solute load. Uric acid stones link to gout.
  • Obstruction → severe colicky flank pain radiating to the groin, hematuria, possible hydronephrosis and infection.
  • Nursing: strain all urine; send recovered stones for analysis.
Memory hook

Strain to explain — catching the stone tells you its type, which guides prevention. Prevention centers on fluids; don't blanket-restrict calcium.

Hydronephrosis & nephrosclerosis

  • Hydronephrosis — fluid backs up and dilates the kidney from obstruction (stones, tumors, scar, prostate enlargement). Risks: infection and loss of function.
  • Nephrosclerosis — thickening/hardening of small renal arteries → ↓ blood flow, ischemia. Think "atherosclerosis of the kidney"; often secondary to hypertension.
Congenital & genetic disorders

Structural & reflux disorders

  • Vesicoureteral reflux — urine flows backward from bladder toward the kidneys, carrying bacteria upward → recurrent infection and damage.
  • Ectopic kidney — abnormal position; significance depends on drainage and function.
  • Horseshoe kidney — the two kidneys are fused; agenesis/hypoplasia = failure to form / to reach normal size.

Polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD)

  • Autosomal dominant, gene on chromosome 16.
  • Multiple fluid-filled cysts replace working nephrons; kidneys enlarge.
  • Often silent until about age 40; progressive toward chronic renal failure. Damage is not reversible.
↔ Health Assessment (NUR 315)

Autosomal dominant = a family-history disorder. A genogram showing an affected parent and ~50% affected children fits the inheritance pattern.

Wilms tumor

Most common kidney tumor in children; large encapsulated mass, usually unilateral, linked to tumor-suppressor defects on chromosome 11. A renal mass in a child needs prompt evaluation — Wilms tumor is commonly malignant.

Acute vs Chronic kidney failurehigh yield

Acute kidney injury (AKI / ARF)

  • Sudden decline in function — often reversible if the cause is treated.
  • Causes: low perfusion/shock, nephrotoxins, contrast, obstruction, severe infection, trauma.
  • Findings: rising creatinine/BUN, fluid retention, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis.
Watch for

Hyperkalemia in AKI is life-threatening (cardiac dysrhythmias). Monitor potassium and ECG closely.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

  • Gradual, usually irreversible loss of nephrons; staged by GFR.
  • Early: may be asymptomatic or ↑ urine output (lost concentrating ability). Later: oliguria.
  • Findings: anemia, fatigue, nausea, hypertension, dry/pruritic skin, easy bruising, fluid/electrolyte problems.
  • Manage potassium and sodium in the diet; avoid nephrotoxins.
The 3 A's of CKD

Azotemia (nitrogen waste ↑), Anemia (↓ EPO), Acidosis (can't excrete acid).

Urine output terms

Oliguria = low output (some urine). Anuria = essentially none. Lecture used < 30 mL/hr as a concerning threshold — but always read it in context.

Dialysis & diuretics

Hemodialysis & the AV fistula

  • Blood is filtered through an external machine, ~3–4 hours, several times a week.
  • Requires vascular access — usually an arteriovenous (AV) fistula joining an artery and vein.
  • Assess the access: feel the thrill (vibration), hear the bruit (whoosh).
Never on the fistula arm

No blood pressure cuff, no tourniquet, no venipuncture or IV. Losing the access threatens the patient's dialysis and survival.

Peritoneal dialysis

  • The peritoneal membrane is the filter; sterile dialysate is instilled through a catheter, dwells, then drains by gravity.
  • Automated form often runs overnight (~10 hrs); offers independence but restricts movement.
  • Major risk: peritonitis — sterile technique is essential.
Concept

Dialysis does not cure kidney disease — it is life-sustaining replacement. In AKI it may be temporary; in CKD it may be long-term until transplant.

Diuretics

Increase excretion of sodium and water to reduce fluid volume — used in hypertension, edema, heart failure, and renal/liver disease. Some are potassium-wasting, some potassium-sparing.

↔ Pharmacology (NUR 312)

K-wasting: loop (furosemide), thiazide → watch for hypokalemia. K-sparing: spironolactone → watch for hyperkalemia. Ties directly to your fluid/electrolyte content.

Endocrine System

Chapter 16 — hormones, feedback, diabetes
Endocrine basics & regulation

How the system is wired

  • General pathway: hypothalamus → pituitary → end organ.
  • Anterior pituitary sends hormones; posterior pituitary releases hormones made by the hypothalamus (ADH, oxytocin).
  • Negative feedback: the end-product hormone suppresses further stimulation (e.g., thyroid hormone suppresses TSH).
  • Receptors adjust: upregulation (more/sensitized) vs downregulation (fewer/less sensitive).

Three ways endocrine glands fail + three levels

  • Deficiency (gland destroyed), excess (tumor/autoimmune), or resistance (receptors don't respond).
  • Primary — problem in the gland itself.
  • Secondary — problem in the pituitary.
  • Tertiary — problem in the hypothalamus.
Memory hook

1-2-3 = gland, pituitary, hypothalamus. Count "up the chain" from the target organ.

The ACTH → cortisol pathway

Hypothalamus (CRH) → anterior pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex (cortisol).

Morning serum cortisol is drawn at 8–9 a.m. because cortisol is normally highest then.

Chain

CRH → ACTH → Cortisol. This one chain explains Addison's, Cushing's, and why steroids can't be stopped abruptly.

Pituitary disorders

Hypopituitarism

  • Loss of one or more pituitary hormones (panhypopituitarism = all).
  • Causes: pituitary tumor, brain surgery, radiation, trauma, ischemia.
  • Signs are nonspecific: weakness, weight change, hypotension, sluggishness, depression, polyuria, dehydration — so hormone levels are needed.
  • Most serious effects: adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes insipidus.

Hyperpituitarism

  • Usually a pituitary adenoma; may over-secrete ACTH, TSH, GH, or prolactin.
  • Prolactinoma is most common → antiestrogenic/antiandrogenic effects.
  • Excess growth hormone → gigantism (children) or acromegaly (adults).
  • Large tumors compress the optic nerve → headaches, visual changes.
ADH disorders: DI vs SIADHhigh yield

Diabetes insipidus (DI)

  • Too little ADH (central) or the kidney won't respond (nephrogenic).
  • Large volume of dilute urine → polyuria, thirst, dehydration, disorientation.
  • Labs: serum osmolality > 300, urine osmolality < 300 mOsm/kg.
  • Treat with desmopressin/vasopressin when appropriate.
Memory hook

DI = Dry Inside. No ADH → water pours out → dehydrated, dilute urine, high serum sodium.

SIADH

  • Too much ADH → fluid retention, concentrated urine, dilute plasma, hypervolemia.
  • Dangerous hyponatremia → cerebral edema.
  • Associated with brain injury, neurosurgery, and small-cell lung cancer.
  • Treat with fluid restriction, slow sodium correction, possible ADH-receptor antagonists.
Memory hook

SIADH = Soaked Inside. Water held in → dilutional hyponatremia → correct sodium slowly to protect the brain.

Thyroid & parathyroidhigh yield

Reading thyroid labs

Classify by T3 and T4. In primary disease, TSH moves the opposite direction.

  • Primary hypothyroid → low T3/T4, high TSH.
  • Primary hyperthyroid → high T3/T4, low TSH.
  • Goiter = enlarged thyroid; can occur with either state or with low iodine.
Memory hook

TSH is the opposite partner in primary disease. Low thyroid hormone → the pituitary shouts louder (↑ TSH).

Hypothyroidism — everything slows & cools

  • Cold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue, lethargy, constipation, bradycardia.
  • Memory deficits, dry skin, hair loss, brittle nails, puffy face.
  • Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the common autoimmune cause; congenital form = cretinism.

Hyperthyroidism — everything speeds & heats

  • Heat intolerance, weight loss, nervousness, insomnia, tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, diarrhea, sweating.
  • Graves' disease is the most common cause → exophthalmos (bulging eyes) and goiter.
Memory hook

Hyper = Hot & fast. Graves' = bug eyes (exophthalmos). Hypo = cold, slow, and heavy.

Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis)

Overwhelming release of thyroid hormone → high fever, tachycardia, agitation, possible psychosis. Often triggered by surgery or trauma.

Medical emergency

Management: block synthesis (methimazole/PTU), block release (iodide), slow the heart (beta-blocker), support circulation (IV glucocorticoids).

Parathyroid (PTH)

  • PTH is released when calcium is low; it raises calcium via bone resorption and intestinal absorption.
  • Hypoparathyroidism — often from inadvertent damage during thyroid surgery → hypocalcemia; treat with calcium + vitamin D.
  • Hyperparathyroidism — usually an adenoma → high PTH and calcium; may need parathyroidectomy.
↔ Concept link

Thyroid surgery risks the neighboring parathyroids → watch for post-op hypocalcemia (tingling, tetany, positive Chvostek/Trousseau).

Adrenal disordershigh yield

Why you never stop steroids abruptly

Long-term glucocorticoids suppress CRH and ACTH → the adrenal gland atrophies. Stopping suddenly leaves the body unable to respond to stress.

Safety

Always taper steroids. Use the smallest effective dose to limit adrenal atrophy.

Addison's disease (primary adrenal insufficiency)

  • Usually autoimmune destruction of the adrenal cortex → too little cortisol/aldosterone.
  • Weakness, hypotension, fatigue, anorexia, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia.
  • Hyperpigmentation (bronzed skin), amenorrhea, loss of axillary/pubic hair.
Memory hook

Addison's = everything down (BP, Na⁺, glucose) — except potassium, which is up. Skin bronzes because ACTH shares a precursor with melanocyte-stimulating hormone.

Cushing's — disease vs syndrome

  • Cushing's disease — pituitary adenoma → high ACTH → high cortisol.
  • Cushing's syndrome — high cortisol from adrenal disease or, most commonly, exogenous steroids.
  • Features: central weight gain, moon face, buffalo hump, striae, easy bruising, poor wound healing, hypertension, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, immune suppression, hirsutism/amenorrhea.
  • Testing: salivary/24-hr urine cortisol; dexamethasone suppression test (no suppression in Cushing's).
Memory hook

Cushing's = everything up. Too much cortisol = the classic "moon face + buffalo hump + thin bruising skin." Disease starts in the pituitary; syndrome is cortisol excess from anywhere (usually steroids).

Pheochromocytoma

Adrenal medulla tumor that secretes catecholamines (epinephrine/norepinephrine). Diagnosis: 24-hr urine for catecholamine metabolites. Treatment: surgical removal + adrenergic blockers.

Classic triad — the 3 P's

Palpitations, Pain (headache), Perspiration, on a background of severe hypertension, tremors, and tachycardia.

Exam focus

A1c reflects ~3 months of average glucose and is preferred for monitoring. Fasting glucose can diagnose but is not enough to judge control.

Classic signs + why they happen

  • Polyuria — glucose spills into urine and drags water out (osmotic diuresis).
  • Polydipsia — cellular dehydration drives thirst.
  • Polyphagia — cells can't use glucose, so hunger rises.
  • Also: blurred vision, recurrent infections, weight change, electrolyte shifts.
The 3 P's

Polyuria, Polydipsia, Polyphagia — pee, drink, eat.

Diabetes labs

  • OGTT (75 g glucose load), fasting/random glucose, A1c, estimated average glucose (eAG).
  • C-peptide and islet-cell antibodies help distinguish T1 from T2.
  • Urinalysis: glucosuria (renal threshold exceeded) and ketonuria (more common in T1).
↔ Pharmacology (NUR 312)

Metformin lowers hepatic glucose output and improves insulin sensitivity — it supplies no insulin, so it is not appropriate for Type 1.

Somogyi = "So low, then high" (a rebound from a nighttime low). Dawn = a steady sunrise rise with no dip.

Hypoglycemia

Glucose < 70 mg/dL from too much insulin, too little food, or extra activity. The body responds with epinephrine and glucagon; the brain is affected quickly, so treat promptly.

Long-term complications & management

Complications of diabetes

  • Macrovascular — atherosclerosis; cardiac events 2–4× more likely.
  • Neuropathy — distal, symmetric; starts in the feet; blunted pain can mask injury and cause a silent MI.
  • Retinopathy — leading cause of adult blindness (microaneurysms, cotton-wool spots).
  • Nephropathy — glomerular damage, microalbuminuria → renal failure.
  • Infection & poor healing — leading cause of nontraumatic lower-limb amputation.
↔ Health Assessment / Fundamentals

This is the "why" behind diabetic foot exams, fundoscopic exams, and meticulous wound/skin care.

Treatment & monitoring goals

  • Primary goal is glycemic control to prevent complications; watch for both hyper- and hypoglycemia.
  • Reduce cardiovascular risk: lipids, blood pressure < 140/90 (T2), smoking cessation, exercise.
  • Clinician tracks A1c + fasting/postprandial glucose; patient self-monitors with a glucometer (before meals, 2 hrs after, bedtime).

Reproductive System & STIs

Chapter 19 — menstrual, prolapse, male, infections
Female menstrual terminology

Menstrual disorder vocabulary

  • Amenorrhea — no menstruation (rule out pregnancy first). Primary = no menses by age 16.
  • Dysmenorrhea — painful periods.
  • Menorrhagia — heavy bleeding lasting > 7 days.
  • Metrorrhagia — bleeding between normal periods.
  • Oligomenorrhea — infrequent periods.
  • Dyspareunia — painful intercourse; menopause ≈ age 51.
Memory hook

Menorrhagia = Много/"more" during periods. Metrorrhagia = "between" (think metro stops in the middle). Oligo = few.

Assessment of bleeding

Take a menstrual history: last menstrual period, duration, interval, regularity, and volume. Saturating a pad or tampon every hour is excessive.

Pregnancy terms & dating

Naegele's rule

Estimate the due date from the last menstrual period: subtract 3 months, add 7 days, add 1 year. Pregnancy runs ~40 weeks from the LMP.

Memory hook

−3 months, +7 days. Conception is ~2 weeks after the LMP if cycles are regular.

Gravidity & parity terms

  • Gravidity = number of pregnancies; parity = deliveries after ~24 weeks.
  • Nulliparous = no pregnancies · Primigravida = first pregnancy · Multiparous = several.
  • G2P2 = two pregnancies, two deliveries.
Infertility & PCOS

Causes of female infertility

  • PCOS — androgen excess, no ovulation.
  • Premature ovarian failure — antibodies against ovarian tissue.
  • Endometriosis — uterine tissue growing outside the uterus.
  • Fallopian tube damage, cervical obstruction, uterine fibroids disrupting implantation.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)

  • Most common endocrine disorder in young women; leading cause of U.S. infertility.
  • Core triad: androgen excess, anovulation, hyperinsulinemia; follicular cysts.
  • Often part of metabolic syndrome; screen for insulin resistance and T2DM.
↔ Endocrine link (this week)

PCOS is where reproductive and endocrine content overlap — metformin can address the insulin-resistance component, the same drug you met in diabetes.

Prolapse & herniation

Uterine prolapse & friends

  • Uterine prolapse — uterus protrudes into the vagina; linked to multiparity, pelvic muscle/nerve injury, obesity, chronic constipation, and menopause.
  • Cystocele — bladder herniates into the vagina.
  • Rectocele — rectum herniates into the vagina.
  • Enterocele — intestine herniates between uterine ligaments.
  • Management: Kegel exercises, a pessary, tissue repair, or hysterectomy.
Memory hook

Cysto = bladder, Recto = rectum, Entero = intestine — named for the organ pushing in.

Cervix, vagina, ovary & breast

Common structural & inflammatory disorders

  • Cervicitis — inflamed cervix from ascending infection; polyps may cause postcoital bleeding.
  • Vaginitis — discharge, burning, itching; bacterial, candida, or trichomonas; identify the organism to treat.
  • Ovarian cysts — follicular (usually asymptomatic, regress) vs corpus luteum (may rupture, cause pain).
  • Breast: mastitis (inflammation, often lactation), galactorrhea (milk without lactation — think prolactinoma).
Male reproductive disordershigh yield

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)

  • Testosterone-driven prostate enlargement that encroaches on the urethra and obstructs flow.
  • Symptoms: hesitancy, frequency, small voids, incomplete emptying, retention (lower urinary tract symptoms).
  • DRE assesses the prostate; PSA helps screen for cancer but cannot diagnose it alone — biopsy confirms.
↔ Renal link (this week)

BPH is a classic cause of urinary retention and obstruction — tying straight back to hydronephrosis and UTI risk in older men.

Erectile dysfunction

Treated with oral PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil), which enhance nitric-oxide vasodilation.

Critical interaction

PDE-5 inhibitors are contraindicated with nitrates — the combination causes severe, dangerous hypotension.

Testicular torsion

Twisting of the spermatic cord cuts off blood flow → sudden, severe, unilateral pain. Most common in adolescents.

Surgical emergency

Detorsion within ~6 hours saves the testicle. Delay risks infarction and loss. This is a urological emergency — no watchful waiting.

Other male anatomy terms

  • Cryptorchidism — undescended testis; correct by age 1 (needs cooler temperature for sperm).
  • Phimosis — foreskin too tight; paraphimosis — foreskin stuck retracted.
  • Priapism — prolonged painful erection (drugs, sickle-cell crisis).
  • Peyronie's — fibrous penile curvature; varicocele/hydrocele/hematocele — scrotal swellings.
Sexually transmitted infectionshigh yield

STI basics & the CDC Five Ps

  • STIs may be bacterial, viral, fungal, or parasitic — and often asymptomatic.
  • Untreated infections can cause pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility.
  • Someone with one STI should be tested for others.
CDC Five Ps of the sexual history

Partners, Practices, Protection (STI prevention), Past history, Pregnancy prevention.

Viral & parasitic STIs

  • HPV — most common STI; types 6/11 cause warts, oncogenic types cause cervical and other cancers. Vaccinate before age 15 (2-dose) or 15–26 (3-dose).
  • HSV — recurrent painful vesicles; no cure; acyclovir reduces symptoms.
  • Trichomonas — most common nonviral STI; green-yellow discharge; treat with metronidazole.

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)

Ascending infection of the upper reproductive tract (often gonorrhea/chlamydia). Causes pelvic pain, fever, discharge, and can lead to infertility. Risk rises with multiple partners, prior STIs, and IUD use.

Signature sign

Chandelier sign — cervical motion tenderness so intense the patient "reaches for the chandelier." Positive on pelvic exam points to PID.
