Renal & Urinary System

Chapter 18Week 8

The kidneys filter waste, balance fluid/electrolytes and acid–base, and help control blood pressure. Read every disorder through three numbers: creatinine, BUN, and GFR, plus what shows up in the urine.

Kidney function, anatomy & urine formation

Foundational
  • The kidneys remove metabolic waste, drugs, and hormones; regulate water, electrolytes, and acid–base; secrete erythropoietin; activate vitamin D; and control BP via the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.
  • Path of urine: kidneys → ureters → bladder → urethra.
  • The nephron is the functional unit. Each kidney has over a million.
  • Three steps: filtration in the renal corpuscle (glomerulus + Bowman capsule), reabsorption of water/nutrients/electrolytes into peritubular capillaries, and secretion.
  • Cells and proteins normally stay in the blood; wastes and dissolved substances pass into the filtrate.
Reabsorption hormones: ADH (posterior pituitary) reabsorbs water; aldosterone (adrenal cortex) reabsorbs sodium in exchange for K⁺/H⁺; atrial natriuretic hormone (heart) reduces sodium and fluid reabsorption.

GFR & its regulation

High-yield
  • GFR (glomerular filtration rate) estimates filtration capacity and is the main way chronic kidney disease is staged.
  • Constricting the afferent arteriole lowers glomerular pressure → less filtrate. Constricting the efferent arteriole raises pressure → more filtrate.
  • Controlled by autoregulation, the sympathetic nervous system, and renin (released by juxtaglomerular cells when afferent blood flow drops).

Key labs: creatinine, BUN, GFR

High-yield
  • Creatinine is muscle waste normally filtered by the kidneys. Elevated serum creatinine is the single most useful indicator of reduced filtration.
  • BUN reflects nitrogenous waste in the blood.
  • Rising BUN and creatinine together suggest reduced clearance — interpret with urine findings, fluid status, and medications.
  • Diabetes, infection, dehydration, obstruction, contrast media, and nephrotoxic drugs can all alter kidney function.

Urinalysis findings

  • Normal urine is straw-colored with mild odor; specific gravity ~1.010–1.050.
  • Proteinuria: glomerular damage / increased permeability.
  • Hematuria: trauma, stones, inflammation, or glomerular disease.
  • Pyuria (WBCs/pus) and bacteriuria: infection.
  • Glucosuria: high blood glucose — and sugar feeds bacteria.
  • RBC casts point to bleeding/inflammation in the nephron (glomerular disease).
  • Odor alone is not diagnostic.

Incontinence & retention

  • Stress incontinence (more common in women): raised intra-abdominal pressure with coughing, lifting, laughing; linked to multiple pregnancies.
  • Overflow incontinence: weakened detrusor in older adults prevents complete emptying.
  • Neurogenic bladder (spastic or flaccid): spinal cord/brain injury interrupts bladder control.
  • Retention: inability to empty; may follow anesthesia or sacral cord injury.
  • Enuresis: involuntary urination in a child older than 4.

Urinary tract infections

High-yield
  • Infection anywhere in the system; most common organism is E. coli. Urine is an excellent growth medium.
  • More common in women (shorter urethra, proximity to anus); in older men, prostatic hypertrophy causes retention.
  • Urethritis (urethra), cystitis (bladder), ureteritis (ureter), pyelonephritis (kidney).
  • Symptoms: dysuria, urgency, frequency, nocturia, cloudy/odorous urine, hematuria. Systemic: fever, malaise, nausea, leukocytosis.
  • Older adults may present with new or worsening confusion.
  • Pyelonephritis adds flank pain and costovertebral angle tenderness; urinary casts appear (tubule involvement).
  • Treat with antibiotics guided by culture & sensitivity; hydrate and empty the bladder regularly.

Glomerulonephritis

High-yield
  • Inflammation of the glomeruli (often post-streptococcal, with ASO antibodies and antigen–antibody complexes activating complement).
  • Increased permeability lets protein and large numbers of RBCs leak into urine; GFR falls, so fluid and waste are retained.
  • Findings: hematuria (dark/cloudy urine), proteinuria, RBC casts, periorbital/facial then general edema, hypertension, flank pain, decreased output, elevated BUN/creatinine.
  • No infection signs (no fever/bacteriuria); complement levels drop.
  • Manage with sodium restriction, fluid/protein control, glucocorticoids, and antihypertensives.
Anchor: Glomerulonephritis = blood (hematuria + RBC casts + hypertension). Nephrotic = protein.

Nephrotic syndrome

High-yield
  • Marked glomerular permeability to protein — massive amounts of plasma protein escape into urine.
  • Findings: heavy proteinuria, low serum albumin (hypoalbuminemia), massive edema, lipiduria (milky urine), urinary casts.
  • Hypoalbuminemia lowers plasma osmotic pressure → generalized edema; aldosterone rises and worsens it.
  • Treat with glucocorticoids, ACE inhibitors (cut protein loss), antihypertensives, and sodium restriction.
Mnemonic — nephrotic is "PALE": Proteinuria (massive), Albumin low, Lipiduria, Edema (massive). Question says low albumin → nephrotic.

Kidney stones (urolithiasis)

High-yield
  • Also called renal calculi / nephrolithiasis. Most common composition is calcium.
  • Insufficient fluid intake is the major factor; also uric acid (gout), struvite, cystine.
  • Symptoms appear with obstruction: severe flank pain / renal colic radiating to the groin, hematuria, nausea, reduced flow, possible hydronephrosis and infection.
  • Nursing: strain all urine; send recovered stones to the lab to identify type.
  • Treat with hydration, ESWL or laser lithotripsy, sometimes surgery; prevent by fluids and diet by stone type (individualize calcium restriction).

Hydronephrosis & nephrosclerosis

  • Hydronephrosis: dilation/fluid buildup in the kidney from impaired drainage (stones, tumors, scar tissue, prostatic enlargement). Risks: impaired function and infection; often silent early.
  • Nephrosclerosis: thickening/hardening of small renal arteries — like atherosclerosis in the kidney. Cuts blood flow and filtration → ischemia; may be primary or secondary to hypertension. Treat with antihypertensives, diuretics, beta blockers, sodium restriction.

Congenital & genetic disorders

  • Vesicoureteral reflux: urine flows backward from bladder toward the kidneys → ascending infection and damage.
  • Agenesis (one kidney fails to form), hypoplasia (undersized), ectopic kidney (abnormal position), horseshoe kidney (fused).
  • Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease: gene on chromosome 16; multiple cysts destroy tissue; usually shows around age 40; progresses to chronic renal failure and can't be reversed.
  • Wilms tumor: most common kidney tumor in children (chromosome 11 suppressor genes); usually unilateral — a renal mass in a child needs prompt evaluation.

Acute kidney injury (AKI / ARF)

High-yield
  • Sudden decline in kidney function that is potentially reversible if the cause is treated.
  • Causes: low perfusion / shock, contrast, nephrotoxic drugs or overdose, obstruction (stones, clots, tumors), pyelonephritis, severe trauma.
  • Consequences: rising creatinine/BUN, fluid retention, hyperkalemia, drug accumulation, metabolic acidosis.
  • Monitor urine output, creatinine, BUN, electrolytes, and pH.
Output terms: oliguria = reduced output (lecture used <30 mL/hr as concerning); anuria = essentially none. Always read a number in the patient's context.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

High-yield
  • Gradual, usually irreversible loss of nephrons; staged by GFR. Early disease is often silent.
  • Early output may rise (lost concentrating ability); later output falls to oliguria.
  • Findings: nausea, fatigue, anemia, weight loss, hypertension, dry/pruritic skin, easy bruising, fluid/electrolyte problems, metabolic acidosis; possible uremic frost.
  • Stage 3 is the key teaching point (diet, meds, avoid further injury); Stage 5 usually needs dialysis or transplant.
  • Restrict potassium, sodium, fluid, and protein; avoid nephrotoxins.
Mnemonic — the 3 A's of CKD: Azotemia (nitrogen waste), Anemia (low erythropoietin), Acidosis.

Dialysis & the AV fistula

High-yield
  • Dialysis replaces filtration/reabsorption — it is life-sustaining, not a cure. May be temporary in AKI or ongoing in end-stage disease.
  • Hemodialysis: blood circulates through an external machine ~3–4 hours, several times a week; needs vascular access, often an AV fistula.
  • An AV fistula connects an artery and vein into a durable vessel; may look enlarged or lumpy.
  • Assess for a thrill (palpable vibration) and a bruit (audible whoosh).
  • Peritoneal dialysis uses the peritoneal membrane; sterile fluid dwells then drains. Major risk is peritonitis. Automated PD often runs overnight (~10 hrs).
⚠ Never place a BP cuff, tourniquet, or IV/venipuncture on the arm with an AV fistula — damage can threaten the patient's dialysis and survival.

Diuretics & diagnostic tests

  • Diuretics increase water/sodium excretion for hypertension, edema, heart/renal/liver disease. Some are potassium-wasting, some potassium-sparing.
  • Cystoscopy uses a scope through the urethra to view the bladder ("cysto-" = bladder, "-oscopy" = scope); can biopsy or remove stones.
  • Culture & sensitivity identifies the organism and effective antibiotic; imaging includes ultrasound, CT, and clearance tests for GFR.

Endocrine System

Chapter 16Week 9

Connect each disorder to its hormone or gland, then decide: too little, too much, or resistance. The general axis is hypothalamus → pituitary → end organ, tuned by negative feedback.

Endocrine regulation & the three failures

Foundational
  • Glands release hormones into blood. Axis: hypothalamus → anterior/posterior pituitary → end organ.
  • Negative feedback: the end-product hormone suppresses further stimulating signals.
  • Three problems: deficiency (gland destroyed — autoimmune, infection, tumor), excess (tumor, autoimmune, mutation), resistance (usually genetic — missing/nonfunctional receptors).
  • Three levels: primary = the gland itself; secondary = the pituitary; tertiary = the hypothalamus.
  • Receptors upregulate (more/more-sensitive) or downregulate (fewer/less-sensitive).
ACTH axis: hypothalamus CRH → anterior pituitary ACTH → adrenal cortex cortisol. Check morning serum cortisol (8–9 a.m.) because cortisol is normally highest then.

Hypo- & hyperpituitarism

  • Hypopituitarism: loss of one or more pituitary hormones (panhypopituitarism = all). Causes: tumor, brain surgery, radiation, trauma, ischemia.
  • Signs are non-specific: weakness, weight change, hypotension, sluggishness, depression, excessive urination, dehydration — so hormone-level testing is required.
  • Most serious: adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes insipidus. Tumor growth can compress the optic nerves (visual disturbances).
  • Hyperpituitarism: usually a pituitary adenoma; prolactinoma is most common; large tumors cause headaches and visual problems.

Diabetes insipidus (DI)

High-yield
  • ADH deficiency or failure to respond → large-volume, dilute urine.
  • Central DI: posterior pituitary doesn't release ADH. Nephrogenic DI: kidneys don't respond to ADH.
  • Signs: polyuria, thirst, dehydration, disorientation.
  • Labs: serum osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality < 300 mOsm/kg.
  • Give ADH (desmopressin/vasopressin): if the kidneys respond, it's central DI.

SIADH

High-yield
  • Excess ADH → fluid retention, concentrated urine, diluted plasma, hypervolemia.
  • Danger: dilutional hyponatremia with cerebral edema.
  • Associated with brain injury, neurosurgery, and small-cell lung cancer (secretes an ADH-like substance).
  • Treat with fluid restriction, slow sodium correction, and possible ADH-receptor antagonists.

Thyroid basics & goiter

High-yield
  • T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 (thyroxine) regulate metabolism; iodine is required to make them.
  • Classify thyroid disorders by T3 and T4; in primary disease, TSH runs opposite to T3/T4.
  • Goiter = enlarged thyroid; can occur with excess TSH, low iodine, or goitrogens — with or without dysfunction.

Hypothyroidism

High-yield
  • Low metabolism — everything slows down.
  • Cold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue, lethargy, constipation, memory deficits, dry skin, hair loss, brittle nails, bradycardia, decreased fertility, puffy face.
  • Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the autoimmune cause (anti-thyroglobulin, anti-TPO antibodies). Congenital form = cretinism.

Hyperthyroidism

High-yield
  • High metabolism — everything speeds up.
  • Heat intolerance, weight loss, nervousness, insomnia, tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, diarrhea, sweating, increased sympathetic activity.
  • Graves' disease is the most common cause (thyroid-stimulating antibodies); may cause exophthalmos (wide-eyed bulging stare) and a goiter.

Thyrotoxic crisis (thyroid storm)

Emergency
  • Overwhelming release of thyroid hormone; free T4/T3 highly elevated, TSH usually undetectable.
  • High fever, tachycardia, agitation, possible psychosis; often precipitated by surgery or trauma.
  • Management: methimazole or PTU (stop synthesis), iodide (block release), beta blocker / calcium-channel blocker (slow the heart), IV glucocorticoids to support circulation.
Recognize it: hyperthyroid signs turned extreme — fever, fast, furious.

Parathyroid disorders

  • Four glands on the back of the thyroid secrete PTH when calcium is low; PTH raises calcium via bone resorption and intestinal absorption.
  • Hypoparathyroidism: often from inadvertent damage during thyroid surgery → hypocalcemia. Treat with calcium and vitamin D.
  • Hyperparathyroidism: primary often from an adenoma → elevated PTH and calcium; may need parathyroidectomy and calcium-lowering treatment.

Adrenal insufficiency (Addison's) & steroid caution

High-yield
  • Addison's = primary adrenal insufficiency, most often autoimmune destruction of the adrenal cortex.
  • Signs: weakness, hypotension, fatigue, anorexia, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hyperpigmentation (tanned look), amenorrhea, loss of pubic/axillary hair.
  • Hyperpigmentation happens because ACTH and MSH share a precursor.
⚠ Never stop steroids abruptly — exogenous glucocorticoids suppress CRH/ACTH and cause adrenal atrophy; sudden withdrawal can trigger adrenal insufficiency. Use the smallest effective dose.

Hyperadrenalism (Cushing)

High-yield
  • Cushing's disease: elevated ACTH, usually a pituitary adenoma. Cushing's syndrome: excess cortisol from adrenal disease or (most commonly) exogenous steroids.
  • Signs: weight gain with central fat, moon facies, buffalo hump, striae, easy bruising, poor wound healing, hypertension, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, immune suppression; women get hirsutism and amenorrhea.
  • Diagnose with salivary/24-hr urine cortisol and the dexamethasone suppression test — no suppression suggests Cushing's.
Direction check: Addison = too little cortisol (hypotension, low Na, high K, low glucose). Cushing = too much (hypertension, high glucose, central obesity).

Pheochromocytoma

  • Adrenal medulla tumor that secretes catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine) → excessive sympathetic stimulation.
  • Hypertension, tremors, tachycardia, increased contractility, arrhythmias.
  • Classic triad: palpitations, headache, profuse sweating.
  • Diagnose with a 24-hour urine for catecholamine metabolites; treat with surgical removal and adrenergic blockers.

Diabetes mellitus — types, values & tests

FoundationalHigh-yield
  • Type 1: autoimmune destruction of beta cells → absent insulin; needs lifelong insulin; often presents with DKA.
  • Type 2 (~90%): insulin resistance linked to obesity, inactivity, dyslipidemia, smoking, genetics; part of metabolic syndrome.
  • Gestational: pregnancy hormones raise resistance; risks macrosomia and later Type 2; screen with 2nd-trimester OGTT.
  • Classic 3 P's: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia. Also blurred vision, recurrent infection, weight change, electrolyte shifts.
  • Metformin lowers intestinal glucose absorption and hepatic glucose output and improves sensitivity — it does not supply insulin, so it is not appropriate for Type 1.

Hypoglycemia

  • Blood glucose < 70 mg/dL; brain function is affected.
  • Causes: too much insulin, too little food, excess activity, infection/illness, drug interactions.
  • The body compensates with epinephrine, glucagon, and sympathetic activation to raise glucose.

Long-term complications of diabetes

  • Atherosclerosis: acute cardiac events are 2–4× more likely; hyperglycemia drives oxidative vascular damage.
  • Peripheral neuropathy: distal, symmetric; starts in the feet; burning/tingling with blunted pain sensation — injuries and even silent MI get missed; Charcot joint.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: tachycardia/hypotension, gastroparesis, UTIs, erectile dysfunction, reduced sweating, blunted hypoglycemia awareness.
  • Nephropathy: glomerular capillary damage, microalbuminuria, RAAS activation → renal failure.
  • Retinopathy: leading cause of adult blindness — microaneurysms, cotton-wool spots, fragile new vessels; needs regular fundoscopic exams.
  • Infection & amputation: reduced WBC function, Candida overgrowth; diabetic foot disease is the top cause of nontraumatic lower-limb amputation.

Reproductive System

Chapter 19Week 9

Female and male reproductive disorders, plus prostate and infertility conditions. Watch the terminology closely — many exam items turn on one word.

Menstrual disorder terminology

High-yield
  • Amenorrhea: absence of menstruation — always rule out pregnancy in childbearing age. Primary = no menses before age 16.
  • Dysmenorrhea: painful menstruation.
  • Menorrhagia: excessive bleeding lasting > 7 days.
  • Metrorrhagia: bleeding outside the normal period.
  • Oligomenorrhea: infrequent periods.
  • Dyspareunia: painful intercourse. Menopause: cessation of cycles, average age 51.

Pregnancy terms & dating

  • Dated in weeks from the first day of the LMP; childbirth is ~40 weeks after LMP.
  • Naegele's rule: add 1 year to the LMP, subtract 3 months, add 7 days.
  • Nulliparous (no pregnancies), multiparous (several), primigravida (first pregnancy), primiparous (post first delivery).
  • Gravidity = pregnancies; parity = deliveries. G2P2 = 2 pregnancies, 2 deliveries.

Uterine & organ prolapse

  • Uterine prolapse: uterus protrudes into the vagina; linked to multiparity, pelvic muscle/nerve injury, obesity, chronic constipation, connective-tissue disorders, and menopausal estrogen loss.
  • Managed with Kegel exercises, a pessary (support device), native-tissue repair, or hysterectomy.
  • Cystocele = bladder into the vagina; rectocele = rectum; enterocele = intestine. Herniation is treated surgically.

Cervix, vagina & ovarian cysts

  • Cervicitis: cervical inflammation from ascending vaginal infection.
  • Vaginitis: inflammation with discharge, burning, itching, painful urination/intercourse; bacterial, candidal, or trichomonal — identify the organism to treat.
  • Follicular cyst: failure to release the ovum; usually asymptomatic, resolves on its own.
  • Corpus luteum cyst: forms after ovulation; often symptomatic and may rupture (pelvic pain, amenorrhea, then irregular bleeding).

Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)

High-yield
  • Most common endocrine disorder in young women and the leading U.S. cause of infertility.
  • Core features: androgen excess, anovulation, hyperinsulinemia, follicular cysts.
  • Often part of metabolic syndrome; evaluate for insulin resistance and Type 2 diabetes.
  • Metformin can address the metabolic/insulin component.

Breast & male anatomy notes

  • Mastitis: breast inflammation, most often during lactation. Galactorrhea: milk in a non-lactating breast — may signal a prolactin-secreting tumor.
  • Cryptorchidism: undescended testes — needs surgical correction (sperm needs lower-than-body temperature).
  • Phimosis (foreskin too tight) vs. paraphimosis (retracted and stuck).
  • Priapism: prolonged, painful erection — linked to sildenafil and sickle cell crisis.

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)

High-yield
  • Testosterone-sensitive prostate enlargement with aging (80% of men ≥ 80) that encroaches on the urethra and obstructs outflow.
  • Lower urinary tract symptoms: hesitancy, frequency, small voids, incomplete emptying, retention.
  • Evaluate with a DRE; PSA helps assess for cancer but cannot diagnose cancer alone.

Erectile dysfunction

High-yield
  • Treated with oral PDE inhibitors (sildenafil/Viagra, tadalafil/Cialis) that enhance nitric oxide and increase penile vasodilation.
⚠ PDE inhibitors are contraindicated with nitrates — the combination can cause severe hypotension.

Testicular torsion

EmergencyHigh-yield
  • Twisting of the spermatic cord obstructs blood flow → ischemia and infarction; most common in adolescents.
  • Sudden, severe, unilateral pain.
  • Urological emergency — surgical correction is required. Detorsion within ~6 hours limits complications; delay risks loss of the testicle.

Sexually Transmitted Infections

Chapter 19Week 9

STIs may be bacterial, viral, fungal, or parasitic and are often asymptomatic. Untreated infection can cause pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility, so anyone with one STI should be tested for others.

STI basics & the CDC Five P's

High-yield
  • Transmitted through sexual contact; some also via blood, pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding.
  • About half of STIs occur in ages 15–24, with rising rates in older adults.
  • Presentation: women may have purulent discharge, bleeding, pelvic pain; men may have urethritis and penile discharge; both can have anogenital/oropharyngeal involvement.
CDC Five P's of the sexual history: Partners, Practices, Prevention of STIs, Past history of STIs, Prevention of pregnancy.

Chlamydia

High-yield
  • One of the most common STIs; often asymptomatic — can silently cause cervicitis, PID, and infertility.
  • Vaginal delivery can give a newborn pneumonia or conjunctivitis.
  • Diagnose by NAAT; treat with antibiotics — reinfection is likely if the partner isn't treated.

Gonorrhea

High-yield
  • Organism: Neisseria gonorrhoeae; 2nd most reported bacterial STI in the U.S.
  • Men: dysuria, purulent discharge, proctitis. Women: often asymptomatic — discharge, PID, cervicitis.
  • Can cause newborn eye infection; notable for antibiotic resistance.

Syphilis

High-yield
  • Organism: Treponema pallidum; treatable but never fully eradicated from the body.
  • Four phases: primary (chancre), secondary (whole-body rash, highly infectious), latent (asymptomatic), tertiary (neurosyphilis, gummas).

Bacterial vaginosis & trichomoniasis

  • Bacterial vaginosis (Gardnerella): thin "fishy" discharge with clue cells; from shifts in vaginal microbiota.
  • Trichomoniasis (Trichomonas vaginalis): most common nonviral STI; green-yellow discharge, pruritus, dysuria. Treat with metronidazole — treat partners too.

HPV & HSV

High-yield
  • HPV: most common STI; types 6 & 11 cause genital warts (condylomata acuminata); other types are oncogenic (cervix, vagina, vulva, penis, anus, throat). Vaccine ideally started before age 15.
  • HSV (genital herpes, HSV-1 or -2): recurrent painful vesicles preceded by burning/erythema; no cure — acyclovir reduces symptoms. HSV ulcers increase HIV risk.

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)

High-yield
  • Ascending infection into the upper reproductive tract; a common cause is N. gonorrhoeae (acute) or C. trachomatis (often silent).
  • Pelvic pain, fever, cervical/vaginal discharge, elevated ESR/CRP.
  • Chandelier sign: exquisite cervical-motion tenderness on pelvic exam.
  • Highest risk with multiple partners, prior STIs, and IUD use; can lead to infertility.
