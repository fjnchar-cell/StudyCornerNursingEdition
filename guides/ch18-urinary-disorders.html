1 · Functions & Anatomy of the Urinary System

A&P

What the urinary system does

The kidneys keep the internal environment stable — far more than "making urine."

  • Removes metabolic wastes (nitrogen wastes like urea and creatinine).
  • Removes hormones, drugs, and other foreign material from the body.
  • Regulates water, electrolyte, and acid–base balance.
  • Secretes erythropoietin → stimulates red blood cell production.
  • Activates vitamin D → needed for calcium absorption and bone health.
  • Regulates blood pressure through the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS).
High-yieldTwo of these functions explain the classic complications of chronic renal failure: no erythropoietin → anemia; no vitamin D activation → hypocalcemia & bone disease.

Structural path of urine

Kidneys→Ureters→Bladder→Urethra

Nephron — the functional unit

  • Each kidney has over a million nephrons.
  • Renal corpuscle = glomerulus + Bowman capsule (site of filtration).
  • Renal tubules in order: proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct.
Mnemonic — tubule order"Please Let Diane Come" = Proximal → Loop of Henle → Distal → Collecting duct.

Blood flow through the kidney

Know that filtration happens between two arterioles — that's what makes GFR controllable.

Renal artery → interlobar → arcuate → interlobular artery → afferent arteriole → glomerular capillaries → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries → interlobular vein → arcuate → interlobar → renal vein.

RememberAfferent arteriole = Arrives (blood in). Efferent = Exits. The glomerulus sits between two arterioles — unique in the body.

2 · Urine Formation, Hormones & GFR

A&P

Three steps of urine formation

① Filtration (in the renal corpuscle)

  • Large volume of fluid passes from glomerular capillaries into Bowman capsule.
  • Wastes, nutrients, electrolytes, and dissolved substances filter through.
  • Cells and proteins stay in the blood — they are too large to filter normally.

② Reabsorption (into peritubular capillaries)

  • Essential nutrients, water, and electrolytes are reclaimed.
  • Transport mechanisms: active transport, co-transport, osmosis (water).
  • Most water and all glucose reabsorption happens in the proximal convoluted tubule.
  • Controls pH and electrolyte balance.

③ Secretion

  • Additional wastes and ions moved from blood into the tubule for excretion.
High-yieldFinding protein or blood cells in urine is abnormal — it means the glomerular filter is leaking (glomerulonephritis, nephrotic syndrome).

Hormones controlling reabsorption

HormoneSourceAction
ADH (antidiuretic hormone)Posterior pituitary↑ water reabsorption in distal tubule & collecting duct → concentrates urine
AldosteroneAdrenal cortex↑ sodium reabsorption in exchange for potassium or hydrogen (water follows Na⁺)
Atrial natriuretic hormone (ANH)Heart (atria)↓ sodium & fluid reabsorption → lowers blood volume
Mnemonic — aldosterone"Sodium retention, Potassium excretion." Aldosterone keeps salt (and water), dumps potassium. ANH does the opposite of aldosterone.

Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)

GFR is controlled by adjusting the diameter of the two arterioles around the glomerulus.

ChangeEffect on glomerular pressureEffect on filtrate/GFR
Constrict afferent arteriole↓ pressure↓ filtrate
Dilate afferent arteriole↑ pressure↑ filtrate
Constrict efferent arteriole↑ pressure↑ filtrate

Three factors controlling arteriolar constriction

  • Autoregulation — local diameter changes in response to renal blood flow.
  • Sympathetic nervous system — increases vasoconstriction in both arterioles.
  • Renin — released by juxtaglomerular cells when afferent blood flow drops → triggers the renin–angiotensin mechanism → raises BP.
High-yieldDecreased GFR is the central problem in most renal disease. It causes retention of nitrogen wastes (↑ BUN & creatinine), fluid, and acid → azotemia, edema, metabolic acidosis.

3 · Incontinence & Retention

A&P

Incontinence — loss of voluntary bladder control

TypeMechanism / who
EnuresisInvoluntary urination in a child older than 4 yrs; linked to developmental delay, sleep patterns, psychosocial factors.
Stress (more common in women)↑ intra-abdominal pressure forces urine past sphincter — coughing, lifting, laughing; multiple pregnancies.
OverflowIncompetent sphincter or weakened detrusor muscle → bladder never fully empties → frequency + leakage; common in older adults.
Neurogenic bladderSpinal cord injury or brain damage disrupts CNS/ANS control; may be spastic or flaccid.

Retention — inability to empty the bladder

  • May be accompanied by overflow incontinence.
  • Sacral-level spinal cord injury blocks the micturition reflex.
  • May follow general or spinal anesthesia.

4 · Diagnostic Tests

Diagnostics

Urinalysis — appearance of urine

FindingMeaning
Straw-colored, mild odor, SG 1.010–1.050Normal urine
CloudyProtein, blood, bacteria, or pus
Dark colorHematuria, excess bilirubin, or highly concentrated urine
Unusual/unpleasant odorInfection, or from diet/medication

Abnormal constituents of urine

FindingSignificance
Hematuria (blood)Small amounts → infection, inflammation, tumor. Large amounts → ↑ glomerular permeability or hemorrhage.
Proteinuria / albuminuriaAlbumin or plasma proteins leaking into filtrate (damaged glomerulus).
BacteriuriaUrinary tract infection.
Urinary castsInflammation of the kidney tubules (upper tract, not just bladder).
Specific gravityAbility of tubules to concentrate urine. Low = dilute; high = concentrated (both judged with normal hydration). Abnormal SG relates to renal failure.
Glucose & ketonesPoorly controlled diabetes mellitus.
High-yield distinctionCasts = kidney tubule involvement. If you see casts, think upper tract (pyelonephritis, glomerulonephritis), not simple cystitis.

Blood tests

  • ↑ Serum urea (BUN) & creatinine → failure to excrete nitrogen wastes, caused by ↓ GFR.
  • Metabolic acidosis → ↓ GFR; tubules fail to control acid–base balance.
  • Anemia → ↓ erythropoietin and/or bone marrow depression.
  • Electrolytes → depend on related fluid balance.
  • Antistreptolysin O (ASO) / antistreptokinase titers → diagnose poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis.
  • ↑ Renin → kidney as a cause of hypertension.

Other tests

  • Culture & sensitivity — identifies the causative organism and guides drug choice.
  • Radiologic — radionuclide imaging, angiography, ultrasound, CT, MRI, IV pyelography (structure, flow, filtration).
  • Clearance tests — creatinine or inulin clearance → assess GFR.
  • Cystoscopy — visualizes lower tract; can biopsy or remove stones.
  • Biopsy — obtains tissue specimen.

5 · Diuretic Drugs

Treatments

Purpose & use

  • Remove excess sodium and water by ↑ excretion through the kidneys.
  • Reduce fluid volume in tissues and blood.
  • Prescribed for renal disease, hypertension, edema, CHF, liver disease, pulmonary edema.
  • Different drugs use different mechanisms to increase urine volume.
High-yield — potassium!Some diuretics are potassium-wasting (loops, thiazides) and some are potassium-sparing (spironolactone). Always monitor serum K⁺. Watch for hypokalemia (wasting) or hyperkalemia (sparing).
Cross-course link · NUR 312 PharmTies directly to your fluid/electrolyte and cardiovascular drug content — loop (furosemide), thiazide (HCTZ), K⁺-sparing (spironolactone), osmotic (mannitol), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

6 · Dialysis

Treatments

Overview

Dialysis performs the kidney's filtration and reabsorption functions when the kidneys can't.

  • Two forms: hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis.
  • Sustains life during kidney failure.
  • Used for acute failure until the primary problem reverses.
  • Used for end-stage failure until a successful kidney transplant is available.

Hemodialysis vs. Peritoneal dialysis

HemodialysisPeritoneal dialysis
SettingHospital, dialysis center, or home w/ special equipment & trainingUsually outpatient; can be done at night or while ambulatory
MembraneArtificial semipermeable membrane in the machinePatient's own peritoneal membrane
How it worksBlood flows from implanted shunt/catheter in an artery → machine (exchange with dialysate) → returned to vein; cells & proteins stay in bloodCatheter implanted in peritoneal cavity; dialysate instilled, then drained by gravity
Time~3 times/week, 3–4 hrs eachTakes more time than hemodialysis; needs loose clothing for the fluid bag
Main complicationsShunt infection, blood clots, sclerosed/damaged vessels; ↑ risk of hepatitis B/C & HIV if Standard Precautions not followedPeritonitis (infection)
High-yieldBoth types: give prophylactic antibiotics, watch for infection, and use caution with many drugs — toxic levels can build up because the kidneys aren't clearing them.

7 · Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs)

Infection

Basics

  • Very common; urine is an excellent growth medium.
  • Lower UTI: cystitis (bladder), urethritis (urethra).
  • Upper UTI: pyelonephritis (kidney).
  • Most common organism: Escherichia coli.
  • Others: Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Serratia, Pseudomonas, Enterococcus, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma.

Why more common in certain groups

  • Women — short urethra + proximity to anus.
  • Older men — prostatic hypertrophy → urine retention.
  • Children — congenital abnormalities.
  • Predisposing: incontinence, urine retention, direct fecal contamination.
Mnemonic — E. coliThe #1 UTI bug comes from the gut — think "E. coli = Enteric, from the colon," which explains why fecal contamination and a short urethra near the anus drive female UTIs.

Cystitis & Urethritis

  • Bladder wall (cystitis) and urethra (urethritis) become inflamed → hyperactive bladder, reduced capacity.
  • Local signs: dysuria, urgency, frequency, nocturia, pelvic pain.
  • Systemic signs may appear: fever, malaise, nausea, leukocytosis.
  • Urine often cloudy w/ unusual odor.
  • Urinalysis: bacteriuria, pyuria, microscopic hematuria.

Two categories of urethritis

  • Gonococcal — caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
  • Nongonococcal (NGU) — most common cause is Chlamydia trachomatis.

Pyelonephritis (upper UTI)

  • Involves one or both kidneys; infection ascends from ureter into kidney.
  • Purulent exudate fills the pelvis and calyces.
  • Recurrent/chronic infection → scar tissue → loss of tubule function.
  • Obstruction + collected filtrate → hydronephrosis → eventual chronic renal failure if untreated.
  • Signs = cystitis signs plus renal pain: dull, aching lower back/flank pain, high fever.
  • Urinalysis similar to cystitis but with urinary casts (tubule involvement).
High-yield differentiatorFlank pain + fever + casts = pyelonephritis (upper). Cystitis stays lower: dysuria/frequency without flank pain or casts.

Treatment of UTIs

Antibiotics selected by culture & sensitivity:

  • Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim, Cotrim, Septra)
  • Nitrofurantoin (Furadantin)
  • Cephalosporins (Keflex, Duricef)
  • Carbapenems (Doribax)
  • Amoxicillin
  • Fosfomycin — used for pregnant women

8 · Inflammatory Disorders: Glomerulonephritis & Nephrotic Syndrome

Inflammation

Glomerulonephritis

An immune (antigen–antibody) process that inflames the glomeruli.

Pathophysiology

  • Antistreptococcal (ASO) antibodies form an antigen–antibody complex.
  • Complex activates the complement system → inflammatory response in glomeruli.
  • ↑ capillary permeability → leakage of protein and large numbers of RBCs.
  • Congestion & cell proliferation → ↓ GFR → retention of fluid and wastes.

Signs & symptoms

  • Dark, cloudy urine.
  • Facial & periorbital edema first, then generalized edema.
  • Elevated blood pressure (↑ renin + ↓ GFR).
  • Flank/back pain (edema stretches the renal capsule).
  • Decreased urine output; general signs of inflammation.

Diagnostics

  • ↑ serum urea & creatinine.
  • ↑ anti-DNase B, ASO, antistreptokinase antibodies.
  • ↓ complement levels (used to track renal inflammation).
  • Metabolic acidosis.
  • Urinalysis: proteinuria, hematuria, erythrocyte (RBC) casts, no evidence of infection.

Treatment

  • Possible sodium restriction; decreased protein & fluid in severe cases.
  • Glucocorticoids to reduce inflammation.
  • Antihypertensives.
High-yield hallmarkRBC casts + hematuria + periorbital edema after a strep infection = poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis. Contrast with nephrotic syndrome below.

Nephrotic Syndrome

  • Glomerular capillaries become abnormally permeable → large amounts of plasma protein escape into filtrate.
  • May be idiopathic in children 2–6 yrs; may be secondary to SLE (lupus), nephrotoxins, or drugs.

Pathophysiology

  • Hypoalbuminemia → ↓ plasma osmotic pressure → generalized edema.
  • Blood pressure stays low/normal (may rise with angiotensin II).
  • ↓ blood volume → ↑ aldosterone → more severe edema.
  • High blood cholesterol; lipiduria gives milky urine.

Signs & symptoms

  • Proteinuria, lipiduria, casts; massive edema; sudden increase in girth.

Treatment

  • Glucocorticoids (reduce inflammation).
  • ACE inhibitors — may decrease protein loss in urine.
  • Antihypertensives; sodium restriction.
Compare: -itis vs. -oticGlomerulonephritis = inflammation, leaks blood (RBC casts, hematuria). Nephrotic syndrome = leaks protein (massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, huge edema, lipiduria). "NephroTic = Tons of proTein."

9 · Urinary Tract Obstructions: Calculi & Hydronephrosis

Obstruction

Urolithiasis (calculi / stones)

  • Can develop anywhere in the urinary tract; small to very large.
  • Tend to form with: excess solutes in filtrate, insufficient fluid intake (major factor), and UTI.
  • Manifestations occur only with obstruction of urine flow.
  • Obstruction may lead to infection and hydronephrosis (dilation of calyces) with renal tissue atrophy.

Stone types

StoneForms with
Calcium salts (most common)High urine calcium; forms readily in alkaline urine
Uric acidHyperuricemia (gout, high-purine diet, chemotherapy); forms in acidic urine
Struvite & cystineDepend on predisposing factors

Signs, treatment & prevention

Signs & symptoms

  • Stones in kidney/bladder are often asymptomatic (found via recurrent infections).
  • Flank pain from renal capsule distention.
  • Renal colic — intense spasms of flank pain radiating to the groin when a stone obstructs the ureter; lasts until stone passes or is removed.
  • Possible nausea/vomiting, cool moist skin, rapid pulse.
  • Radiology confirms stone location.

Treatment

  • Small stones may pass on their own.
  • Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL); laser lithotripsy.
  • Drugs to partially dissolve stones; surgery.

Prevention

  • Treat the underlying condition.
  • Adjust urine pH through diet.
  • Consistent increased fluid intake — the single biggest preventive measure.
High-yield NCLEXThe #1 patient teaching for stone prevention is increase fluid intake. And renal colic pain radiates from flank to groin.

Hydronephrosis

  • A secondary problem — dilation of the kidney behind an obstruction.
  • Causes: complication of calculi, tumors/scar tissue, untreated prostatic enlargement, developmental abnormalities.
  • Frequently asymptomatic early.
  • Diagnosed by ultrasonography, radionuclide imaging, CT, or renal scan.
  • If the cause is not removed → chronic renal failure.

10 · Tumors: Renal Cell & Bladder Cancer

Tumors

Renal Cell Carcinoma

  • Primary tumor from tubule epithelium, most often in the renal cortex.
  • Often asymptomatic early; frequently already metastasized (liver, lung, bone, CNS) at diagnosis.
  • More frequent in men and smokers.
  • Treatment: removal of the kidney; immunotherapy in some cases.
  • Tumor is radioresistant; chemotherapy usually not used.

Manifestations

  • Painless hematuria (gross or microscopic) — often the first sign.
  • Dull, aching flank pain; palpable mass.
  • Unexplained weight loss; anemia or erythrocytosis.
  • Paraneoplastic syndromes — hypercalcemia or Cushing's syndrome.

Bladder Cancer

  • Most bladder tumors are malignant, arising from transitional epithelium; often multiple tumors.
  • Diagnosed by urine cytology and biopsy.
  • Early signs: hematuria, dysuria; infection is common.
  • Invasive through the wall; metastasizes to pelvic lymph nodes, liver, bone.

Predisposing factors

  • Chemical exposure — aniline dyes, rubber, aluminum (lab/industry work).
  • Cigarette smoking.
  • Recurrent infections; heavy analgesic intake.

Treatment

  • Surgical resection; chemotherapy and radiation; photoradiation in some early cases.
High-yieldPainless hematuria is the classic red flag for BOTH renal cell and bladder cancer. Smoking is a shared risk factor.

11 · Vascular & Congenital Disorders

Vascular/Congenital

Nephrosclerosis (vascular)

  • Vascular changes in the kidney; some occur normally with aging.
  • Thickening/hardening of arteriole & small artery walls → narrowed lumen → ↓ blood supply.
  • ↓ blood supply → stimulates renin → ↑ blood pressure → continued ischemia → tissue destruction → chronic renal failure.
  • Can be a primary lesion or secondary to essential hypertension.

Treatment

  • Antihypertensives, diuretics, beta blockers, reduced sodium intake.
Vicious cycleHypertension damages kidneys → damaged kidneys release renin → renin raises BP further. Breaking the cycle = BP control.

Congenital disorders

DisorderDescription
Vesicoureteral refluxBackflow of urine from bladder toward kidney
AgenesisFailure of one kidney to develop
HypoplasiaFailure to develop to normal size
Ectopic kidneyKidney & ureter displaced from normal position
"Horseshoe" kidneyFusion of the two kidneys

Adult Polycystic Kidney Disease

  • Autosomal dominant gene on chromosome 16.
  • No signs in childhood/young adulthood; first manifestations usually around age 40.
  • Multiple cysts in both kidneys → enlargement → compression & destruction of tissue → chronic renal failure.
  • Diagnosed by abdominal CT or MRI.

Wilms' Tumor

  • Most common tumor in children.
  • Defects in tumor suppressor genes on chromosome 11.
  • May occur with other congenital disorders; usually unilateral.
  • Large encapsulated mass; pulmonary metastases may be present at diagnosis.
Chromosome tipPolycystic kidney → chromosome 16. Wilms' tumor → chromosome 11.

12 · Renal Failure — Acute & Chronic

Renal Failure

Acute Renal Failure (ARF)

Causes

  • Acute bilateral kidney disease.
  • Severe, prolonged circulatory shock or heart failure (ischemia).
  • Nephrotoxins — drugs, chemicals, toxins.
  • Occasional mechanical obstruction — calculi, blood clots, tumors block flow beyond the kidneys.

Features & treatment

  • Sudden onset.
  • Blood tests: ↑ BUN & creatinine, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia.
  • Treatment: identify & remove/treat the primary problem to minimize necrosis and permanent damage.
  • Dialysis to normalize body fluids and maintain homeostasis.
High-yield — ARF is often reversibleBecause ARF has a sudden, identifiable cause, treating it early can restore kidney function. Contrast with chronic failure, which is gradual and irreversible.

Chronic Renal Failure (CRF)

  • Gradual, irreversible destruction of the kidneys over a long time.
  • Asymptomatic in early stages.
  • Causes: chronic kidney disease, congenital polycystic disease, systemic disorders, long-term low-level nephrotoxin exposure.

Stages

StageKey features
1. Decreased renal reserve↓ GFR; higher-than-normal serum creatinine; no clinical symptoms
2. Renal insufficiencyGFR ~20% of normal; significant retention of nitrogen wastes; large volumes of dilute urine; ↓ erythropoiesis; ↑ BP
3. End-stage renal failureNegligible GFR; fluid/electrolytes/wastes retained; azotemia, anemia, acidosis (the 3 A's); all body systems affected; marked oliguria/anuria; needs regular dialysis or transplant
Mnemonic — end-stageThe 3 A's of end-stage renal failure: Azotemia (nitrogen waste buildup), Anemia (no erythropoietin), Acidosis (tubules can't manage acid).

Progression of signs in CRF

Early sign

  • Increased urinary output (kidneys lose concentrating ability first).

General signs

  • Anorexia, nausea, anemia, fatigue, unintended weight loss, exercise intolerance, bone marrow depression & impaired cell function, elevated blood pressure.

Complete failure

  • Oliguria.
  • Dry, pruritic, hyperpigmented skin; easy bruising; possible uremic frost.
  • Peripheral neuropathy; encephalopathy.
  • Impotence (men), menstrual irregularities (women).
  • CHF, dysrhythmias; failure to activate vitamin D; systemic infections.
High-yield trapCRF starts with increased urine output (dilute urine) and ends with oliguria/anuria. Don't assume renal failure always means low urine output.

Diagnostics & treatment (CRF)

Diagnostic findings

  • Decompensated metabolic acidosis; azotemia; severe anemia.
  • Electrolytes vary with water retention — usually hyponatremia & hyperkalemia, plus hypocalcemia & hyperphosphatemia.

Treatment

  • All body systems affected → hard to maintain fluid/electrolyte/acid–base homeostasis.
  • Drugs to stimulate erythropoiesis; drugs for cardiovascular problems.
  • Restrict fluid, electrolytes, and protein intake.
  • Dialysis or transplantation.
Electrolyte pattern to memorizeCRF: Na↓, K↑, Ca↓, Phosphate↑. (Low sodium & calcium; high potassium & phosphate.)
